Between 2020 and 2024, 1,151 people died in 1,042 fatal crashes in Georgia that involved at least one large truck. Those crashes were 13.6% of the 7,673 fatal crashes recorded in the state over the five years. Most of the people killed were not in the truck.

This report counts every truck-involved fatal crash in Georgia in the federal government’s crash census for those years. The crash-level data file and the analysis code are published with it, so every figure below can be checked.

The share has barely moved

[roden_chart name="ga-truck-by-year"]

The number of deaths in truck-involved crashes peaked at 271 in 2022 and was 203 in 2024. Measured as a share of all fatal crashes in Georgia, truck involvement stayed in a narrow band, between 11.9% and 14.5% in every year. Year-to-year movements are small enough that no trend should be read into them.

The 2024 figure comes from the newest annual file, which may still be revised as cases are completed.

82% of those killed were outside the truck

Of the 1,151 deaths, 947 were people who were not in a large truck: 806 were in other vehicles, 131 were pedestrians and 8 were cyclists. The remaining 204 deaths, 18%, were occupants of the truck itself.

[roden_chart name="ga-truck-who-died"]

The pattern holds when the count is restricted to tractor-trailers. In the 623 fatal crashes involving a truck-tractor, 82% of the 707 people killed were outside the truck.

Interstates carry a disproportionate share

Interstates accounted for 15% of Georgia’s fatal crashes but 30% of its truck-involved fatal crashes: 316 crashes. On an interstate, 28% of fatal crashes involved a large truck. On US and state routes the figure was 16%, and on county roads and city streets it was 5%.

By total, I-75 recorded the most: 98 truck-involved fatal crashes and 119 deaths. The two interstates that serve Savannah had the highest truck shares of any Georgia interstate with at least 20 fatal crashes: a large truck was involved in 45% of fatal crashes on I-95 (32 of 71) and 43% on I-16 (26 of 60). Those are small numbers, and a few crashes either way would move the percentages noticeably.

More than half of truck-involved fatal crashes, 600 of them, happened on US and state routes. This report does not rank those roads individually. In Georgia every US highway also carries a state-route number, and the crash records use one label in some years and the other in others, so a ranking would split a single road into two.

Daytime and weekdays

Truck-involved fatal crashes follow the working week. 61% happened between 6 a.m. and 6 p.m., against 42% of fatal crashes that involved no large truck. 83% happened Monday to Friday, against 65% of other fatal crashes, which peak in the evening and on Saturdays and Sundays.

That difference is what freight traffic would predict. It is a description of when these crashes occur, not a finding about why.

The four largest totals are in the core metro Atlanta counties. The highest shares in the list are in Bartow and Douglas counties, which sit on I-75 and I-20 respectively. In Chatham County, which includes Savannah, 26 truck-involved fatal crashes killed 30 people.

Rural roads carried more than their share. 45% of truck-involved fatal crashes happened in rural areas, against 36% of all fatal crashes in the state. On rural roads, 17% of fatal crashes involved a large truck; on urban roads, 12%.

Work zones

42 truck-involved fatal crashes, 4% of the total, were recorded in a work zone. They killed 48 people. That is a small number, but it is 29% of the 144 fatal crashes recorded in Georgia work zones over the period. Work-zone status depends on the reporting officer noting it, so these counts may be incomplete.

What this does and doesn’t show

  • Fatal crashes only. The source records only crashes in which someone died within 30 days. Injury and property-damage crashes involving trucks are far more numerous and are not counted here.
  • Counts, not rates. There is no figure here for miles driven by trucks on each road or in each county. A road or county that carries more freight will record more truck-involved crashes without necessarily being more dangerous per mile.
  • Involvement is not fault. A crash is counted when a large truck was present. The data does not say who caused it. A truck-involved crash may have been caused by the truck driver, by another driver, by the road or by the conditions.
  • A broad definition of truck. “Large truck” means any vehicle over 10,000 pounds gross vehicle weight rating, which includes box trucks, heavy pickups and other single-unit trucks as well as tractor-trailers. Tractor-trailers were involved in 60% of the crashes counted here, and that subset is reported separately where it matters.
  • Recent data may change. Figures for 2024 come from the newest annual file and may be revised.

Methodology

Data. NHTSA Fatality Analysis Reporting System (FARS), national annual files for 2020 through 2024, downloaded 30 September 2026. FARS is the federal census of fatal traffic crashes on US public roads.

Scope. Every fatal crash recorded in Georgia in those files. A crash is counted as truck-involved when at least one vehicle carries a FARS body-type code between 60 and 79, NHTSA’s medium and heavy vehicle range. The tractor-trailer subset is body type 66.

Deaths are people recorded with a fatal injury in the FARS person file, checked crash by crash against the crash-level death count. A death is attributed to a truck occupant when the person was in a vehicle in the large-truck range, and to another vehicle, a pedestrian or a cyclist otherwise.

Road type uses the FARS route signing field. US and state routes are combined because Georgia’s coding of the two varies from year to year. Rural and urban come from the FARS land-use field. Daytime means a crash hour from 6 a.m. to 5:59 p.m.

Reproducing this. The analysis script is published with the report. Point it at the extracted FARS files and it regenerates every number here, and the dataset below. If the output differs from this page, this page is wrong and we would like to know.

The data

The crash-level dataset has one row per truck-involved fatal crash in Georgia, 1,042 rows, with year, FARS case number, county, deaths by person type, truck body types, road, route type, land use, hour, day of week and work-zone status. Download it here: ga-truck-fatal-crashes-2020-2024.csv. It may be reused with attribution.

This report is intended to be updated each year as a new FARS file is released.
