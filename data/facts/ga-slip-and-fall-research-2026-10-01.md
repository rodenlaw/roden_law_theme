### §§ 51-3-1, 51-3-2, 51-3-3 (duty by entrant status)
Source: FindLaw via r.jina.ai, read 2026-10-01, "Current as of March 28, 2024".
- § 51-3-1: `https://codes.findlaw.com/ga/title-51-torts/ga-code-sect-51-3-1/`
  > "Where an owner or occupier of land, by express or implied invitation, induces or leads others to
  > come upon his premises for any lawful purpose, he is liable in damages to such persons for injuries
  > caused by his failure to exercise ordinary care in keeping the premises and approaches safe."
- § 51-3-2: `https://codes.findlaw.com/ga/title-51-torts/ga-code-sect-51-3-2/`
  > "(a) A licensee is a person who: (1) Is neither a customer, a servant, nor a trespasser; (2) Does not
  > stand in any contractual relation with the owner of the premises; and (3) Is permitted, expressly or
  > impliedly, to go on the premises merely for his own interests, convenience, or gratification.
  > (b) The owner of the premises is liable to a licensee only for willful or wanton injury."
- § 51-3-3: `https://codes.findlaw.com/ga/title-51-torts/ga-code-sect-51-3-3/`
  > "(a) As used in this Code section, the term 'possessor of land' means the landowner, occupant of the
  > land, holder of any easement to the land, or lessee of the land. (b) A lawful possessor of land owes
  > no duty of care to a trespasser except to refrain from causing a willful or wanton injury. (c) Georgia
  > common law as it exists and is applied to the doctrine of attractive nuisance, in effect as of
  > January 1, 2014, shall not be construed to be altered by this Code section. (d) This Code section
  > shall not affect any immunities from or defenses to civil liability to which a lawful possessor of
  > land may be entitled."
- **SB 68 does not amend §§ 51-3-1 to 51-3-3.** Its Section 6 adds a new Article 5 to Chapter 3 and
  nothing else in the chapter (bill text lines 172-176: "amended in Chapter 3 ... by adding a new
  article").

### § 51-12-1.1 (medical special damages; new 2025), verbatim from SB 68 § 7
> "(a) In any civil action to recover damages resulting from injury or death to a person, special
> damages for medical and healthcare expenses shall be recoverable only as provided in this Code section.
> (b) Special damages for medical and healthcare expenses shall be limited to the reasonable value of
> medically necessary care, treatment, or services, and the amount of such special damages shall be
> determined by the trier of fact.
> (c) If the plaintiff in any such civil action has any form of public or private health insurance,
> including benefits under a governmental workers' compensation program, evidence relevant to the
> determination of the reasonable value of medically necessary care, treatment, or services pursuant to
> subsection (b) of this Code section shall include both the amounts charged for past, present, or
> future medical and healthcare expenses and the amounts actually necessary to satisfy such charges
> pursuant to the insurance contract or the applicable governmental workers' compensation program,
> regardless of whether the health insurance has been used, is used, or will be used to satisfy such
> charges."
- (d): under a letter of protection, the letter, itemized coded charges, any sale of the receivable (buyer
  and price), and the referral source are "relevant and discoverable".
- (e): abrogates the collateral source rule "to the extent necessary to introduce the evidence described
  in this Code section", while the court may still instruct the jury on collateral source payments.
- (f): either side may still challenge the reasonableness of expenses or the medical necessity of
  treatment.
- **When it applies.** SB 68 § 9(b): "Sections 6 and 7 of this Act shall apply only with respect to
  causes of action arising on or after the effective date of this Act, and any prior causes of action
  shall be governed by prior law." Effective 2025-04-21 (Act 9). For a fall, the cause of action
  ordinarily arises on the date of the fall, so a fall on or after 2025-04-21 is under § 51-12-1.1; an
  earlier fall is under prior law. (The "arises on the date of the fall" gloss is for Tyler.)
- Currency: Justia 2025 history, read 2026-09-30: "enacted by Ga. L. 2025, p. 19, § 7/SB 68, effective
  April 21, 2025." No later act recorded.

### § 9-10-184 (noneconomic damages argument; rewritten 2025), from SB 68 § 1
- (a)(1) economic damages include "past and future medical expenses; costs of rehabilitation; costs of
  therapy; loss of wages; loss of income; loss of earning capacity; loss of services ...".
- (a)(2) noneconomic damages include "physical or emotional pain, discomfort, anxiety, hardship,
  distress, suffering, inconvenience, physical impairment, mental anguish, disfigurement, loss of
  enjoyment of life, loss of society and companionship, loss of consortium, injury to reputation ...".
- (b): "counsel shall not argue the worth or monetary value of noneconomic damages, and counsel shall
  not, in the hearing of the jury or any prospective juror, elicit any testimony regarding, or make any
  reference to, any specific amount or range of amounts of noneconomic damages, the measure of such
  damages being the enlightened conscience of an impartial jury", except under (c).
- (c)(1): counsel may argue a value "only after the close of evidence and at the time of such party's
  first opportunity to argue the issue of damages", "rationally related to the evidence".
- Applies to cases pending on 2025-04-21 (SB 68 § 9(b), "all other provisions").
- Currency: Justia 2025 history, read 2026-09-30: "Ga. L. 1960, p. 174, § 1; Ga. L. 2025, p. 19,
  § 1/SB 68, effective April 21, 2025."
- **Caution:** the flattened PDF ends (e) with "In the trial of a civil action for personal injuries,
  counsel shall be allowed to argue the worth or monetary value of pain and suffering to the jury ...".
  That is the pre-2025 wording of § 9-10-184 and is almost certainly struck text that `pdftotext`
  flattened. **Do not quote it.** Not confirmed against the codified text today (Justia blocked).
- **What the writer must avoid:** any sentence saying a lawyer will "tell the jury what your pain is
  worth", that pain and suffering is computed with a multiplier, or that a jury is given a dollar range.
  "The jury decides what pain and suffering is worth" is consistent with (b)'s "enlightened conscience of
  an impartial jury".

### §§ 51-3-50 to 51-3-57 (negligent security, new 2025): scope confirmed; does not reach slip-and-fall
From SB 68 § 6, read 2026-10-01.
- § 51-3-50(1): "'Negligent security' means any claim against an owner or occupier, or against a
  security contractor, that: (A) Sounds in tort or nuisance, including, but not limited to, any claim
  under Article 1 of this chapter; (B) Seeks to recover damages for bodily injury or wrongful death; and
  (C) Arises from an alleged failure to keep the premises and approaches safe from the wrongful conduct
  of third persons."
- § 51-3-50(8): "Wrongful conduct" is a crime (felony or misdemeanor) or "an intentional, or willful and
  wanton, tort".
- § 51-3-53(a): the article is "the sole and exclusive remedy for negligent security"; (c): it does not
  affect "any other claim, remedy, or cause of action not arising under Article 1".
- **Result:** a fall caused by a spill, a defect, ice, a stair or lighting is a claim about the
  *condition* of the premises, not about a third person's wrongful conduct, so it stays under
  § 51-3-1 and the case law below. Article 5 matters only if a third person's crime or intentional tort
  caused the fall (for example, a fall during an assault). Its elements (particularized warning; prior
  similar incidents on the premises or "within 500 yards"; a specific known physical condition) do
  **not** apply to an ordinary slip-and-fall.
- Applies only to causes of action arising on or after 2025-04-21 (SB 68 § 9(b)).
- Recommended: one sentence at most, or nothing. DO NOT import the 500-yard or "particularized warning"
  elements into this guide.

## 2. The Georgia slip-and-fall proof standard (CASE LAW, PENDING TYLER)

Opinion texts were read on CourtListener (through r.jina.ai) on 2026-10-01. Citations were checked
against CourtListener's citation index. Subsequent history (whether any point has been narrowed since)
was **not** checked with a citator; Tyler confirms.

### 2.1 The two-part test. CASE LAW, PENDING TYLER
- **Robinson v. Kroger Co., 268 Ga. 735, 493 S.E.2d 403 (1997).** Supreme Court of Georgia, "Decided
  December 3, 1997". Citation and year **verified**.
  `https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1292587/robinson-v-kroger-co/`
  > "Finally, we reaffirm that, in order to recover for injuries sustained in a slip-and-fall action, an
  > invitee must prove (1) that the defendant had actual or constructive knowledge of the hazard; and
  > (2) that the plaintiff lacked knowledge of the hazard despite the exercise of ordinary care due to
  > actions or conditions within the control of the owner/occupier. However, the plaintiff's
  > evidentiary proof concerning the second prong is not shouldered until the defendant establishes
  > negligence on the part of the plaintiff — i.e., that the plaintiff intentionally and unreasonably
  > exposed self to a hazard of which the plaintiff knew or, in the exercise of ordinary care, should
  > have known."
  - Also: an invitee's failure to look where she stepped does not, as a matter of law, show a lack of
    ordinary care; "the issue is whether, taking into account all the circumstances existing at the time
    and place of the fall, the invitee exercised the prudence the ordinarily careful person would use in
    a like situation."
  - The court ties the test to § 51-3-1 and to the owner's "superior knowledge of the hazard" (quoting
    Alterman Foods v. Ligon, 246 Ga. 620 (1980)).
  - Distraction doctrine: "one is not bound to the same degree of care in discovering or apprehending
    danger in moments of stress or excitement or when the attention has been necessarily diverted",
    "particularly true where the distraction is placed there by the defendant".
- **American Multi-Cinema, Inc. v. Brown, 285 Ga. 442, 679 S.E.2d 25 (2009).** Supreme Court of
  Georgia, No. S08G1934, "Decided June 1, 2009". Citation and year **verified**.
  `https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1288190/american-multi-cinema-inc-v-brown/`
  > "The plaintiff must plead and prove that: (1) the defendant had actual or constructive knowledge of
  > the hazard; and (2) the plaintiff, despite exercising ordinary care for his or her own personal
  > safety, lacked knowledge of the hazard due to the defendant's actions or to conditions under the
  > defendant's control."
  - It describes Robinson as "our seminal 1997 decision" and restates the burden-shifting: once the
    plaintiff shows evidence of actual or constructive knowledge, "the burden of production shifts to the
    defendant to produce evidence that the plaintiff's injury was caused by his or her own voluntary
    negligence (intentional disregard of a known risk) or causal negligence (failure to exercise ordinary
    care for one's personal safety)", and then back to the plaintiff.
  - Facts (useful as an illustration): a fallen "Wet Floor" sign hidden by an exiting movie crowd. The
    court held a jury could find the theater liable; it declined to read "constructive knowledge" to
    excuse employees who could not see the hazard for the same reason the patron could not.
- **Neutral statement for the writer:** "Georgia courts generally require a person hurt in a fall on a
  business's property to show two things: that the owner knew, or should have known, about the hazard,
  and that the injured person did not know about it despite using ordinary care, because of something
  the owner did or controlled."

### 2.2 How constructive knowledge is shown. CASE LAW, PENDING TYLER
- **Shepard v. Winn Dixie Stores, Inc., 241 Ga. App. 746, 527 S.E.2d 36 (1999)** (Ct. App., Dec. 7,
  1999). `https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1410910/shepard-v-winn-dixie-stores-inc/`
  > "To establish constructive knowledge, Shepard must show that (1) a Winn Dixie employee was in the
  > immediate area of the hazard and could have easily seen the substance or (2) the foreign substance
  > remained long enough that ordinary diligence by Winn Dixie should have discovered it."
  > "Constructive knowledge may be inferred when there is evidence that the owner lacked a reasonable
  > inspection procedure. In order to prevail at summary judgment based on lack of constructive
  > knowledge, the owner must demonstrate not only that it had a reasonable, inspection program in place,
  > but that such program was actually carried out at the time of the incident."
  > "... the plaintiff need not show how long the hazard had been present unless the owner has
  > demonstrated its inspection procedures."
- Same two-route formula appears in J. H. Harvey Co. v. Reddick, 240 Ga. App. 466, 522 S.E.2d 749
  (1999) and Straughter v. J. H. Harvey Co., 232 Ga. App. 29, 500 S.E.2d 353 (1998) (located through
  CourtListener search; **not read**).
- **Neutral statement:** "An owner can be charged with knowledge it did not actually have, for example
  where an employee was close enough to see and remove the hazard, or where the hazard was there long
  enough that a reasonable inspection would have found it." Inspection and sweep logs are therefore
  core evidence (a practical point the writer can make without a citation).

### 2.3 Static conditions, plain view and prior traversal. CASE LAW, PENDING TYLER
- **Robinson** on "plain view": the doctrine "that one is under a duty to look where he is walking and to
  see large objects in plain view which are at a location where they are customarily placed and expected
  to be" had "undergone radical transmutation"; Robinson disapproved decisions holding "as a matter of law
  that an invitee's failure to see before falling the hazard which caused the invitee to fall constitutes
  a failure to exercise ordinary care."
- **McLemore v. Genuine Parts Co., 313 Ga. App. 641, 722 S.E.2d 366 (2012)** (Ct. App., Jan. 24, 2012).
  `https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2501531/mclemore-v-genuine-parts-co/`
  > "If nothing obstructs the invitee's ability to see the static condition, the proprietor may safely
  > assume that the invitee will see it and will realize any associated risks."
- **Perkins v. Val D'Aosta Co., 305 Ga. App. 126, 699 S.E.2d 380 (2010)** (Ct. App., July 9, 2010).
  `https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2393179/perkins-v-val-daosta-co/`
  > "a claim involving a static defect differs from other slip and fall cases in that when a person has
  > successfully negotiated an alleged dangerous condition on a previous occasion, that person is
  > presumed to have equal knowledge of it and cannot recover for a subsequent injury resulting
  > therefrom." (quoting Trans-Vaughn Dev. Corp. v. Cummings, 273 Ga. App. 505 (2005))
  > "[t]he rule imputing knowledge of a danger to a person who has successfully negotiated an alleged
  > dangerous condition before applies only to cases involving a static condition that is 'readily
  > discernible' to a person exercising reasonable care for his own safety." (quoting Rutherford v.
  > Revco Discount Drug Centers, 301 Ga. App. 702 (2009))
  - In Perkins the court reversed summary judgment for the owner (curb, poor lighting, no paint).
- **Brixmor New Chastain Corners SC, LLC v. James, 318 Ga. 17, 896 S.E.2d 544 (2023)** (Supreme Court).
  The petition raised the prior traversal rule, but the Court granted review "not to address these
  issues" and decided a procedural point. **Do not cite it for prior traversal.**
- **Neutral statement:** "Fixed conditions, like a curb or a step, are treated differently from spills.
  If you could plainly see a fixed condition, or had safely walked over it before, Georgia courts may
  presume you knew about it, though that presumption is limited to conditions that were readily visible."
- Not a slip-and-fall rule to state: the live practice page's "the owner still had a duty to remedy a
  known danger despite its visibility" (see § 5).

### 2.4 Entrant status (statute plus case law). STATUTE-TEXT VERIFIED / CASE LAW, PENDING TYLER
- The two-part test above is the **invitee** standard. A licensee can recover "only for willful or wanton
  injury" (§ 51-3-2(b)); a trespasser is owed no duty "except to refrain from causing a willful or wanton
  injury" (§ 51-3-3(b)).
- **Social guests.** Georgia cases treat a social guest in a home as a licensee (lead: Wren v. Harrison,
  165 Ga. App. 847, 303 S.E.2d 67 (1983); located through CourtListener search for "social guest" with
  "is a licensee" and "51-3-2"; **not read**). CASE LAW, PENDING TYLER. This matters because the live
  blog says the opposite (§ 5).
- Recommendation: the guide should focus on invitees (customers, business visitors) and say in one
  sentence that someone visiting for their own purposes, or a trespasser, is owed less and has a harder
  case.

### 2.5 How the second prong interacts with § 51-12-33. FOR TYLER
- Robinson's second prong can defeat the claim outright (a plaintiff who knew, or should have known, of
  the hazard). § 51-12-33 apportions fault and bars only at 50%. § 51-11-7 bars recovery where ordinary
  care would have avoided the consequences.
- How a consumer page should describe the three together is a judgment call. The live slip-and-fall
  page's "two fault defenses" paragraph (50% apportionment bar plus § 51-11-7) is the current Roden
  framing. Q5 asks Tyler.

## 3. What drives value (law-supported only; no dollar figures, averages or ranges)

1. **Liability first.** Without proof of the owner's actual or constructive knowledge (§ 2.1, § 2.2),
   there is no recovery however serious the injury. Entrant status sets the duty (§§ 51-3-1 to -3).
2. **Medical expenses.** § 9-10-184(a)(1) lists "past and future medical expenses; costs of
   rehabilitation; costs of therapy" as economic damages.
   - **Fall on or after 2025-04-21:** § 51-12-1.1 limits medical specials to "the reasonable value of
     medically necessary care, treatment, or services", decided by the jury; if the injured person has
     health insurance, the evidence includes both what was charged and what was "actually necessary to
     satisfy such charges", whether or not the insurance was used. Treatment under a letter of
     protection opens the arrangement to discovery ((d)).
   - **Fall before 2025-04-21:** prior law governs (SB 68 § 9(b)). The guide should not describe prior
     law's measure (not researched here).
   - Tyler said on 2026-09-30 that the WD guide need not mention § 51-12-1.1. A slip-and-fall value guide
     is different: medical bills are its main economic item, and "your medical bills" is the sentence a
     writer is most likely to overstate. Q3 asks him again for this guide.
3. **Lost wages and earning capacity.** § 9-10-184(a)(1): "loss of wages; loss of income; loss of earning
   capacity". No 2025 change.
4. **Pain and suffering.** § 9-10-184(a)(2) lists "physical or emotional pain, discomfort, anxiety,
   hardship, distress, suffering, inconvenience, physical impairment, mental anguish, disfigurement, loss
   of enjoyment of life". The measure is "the enlightened conscience of an impartial jury" (§ 9-10-184(b)).
   This research found no statute capping noneconomic damages in a premises case against a private
   owner, but did not search for one exhaustively; the pack's only related item (Nestlehutt, striking
   the § 51-13-1 medical-malpractice cap) is PENDING and malpractice-specific. So the guide says "Georgia
   law sets no formula" and says "no cap" only if Tyler approves it (Q6). **No multiplier.**
5. **Your share of fault.** § 51-12-33: reduced by your percentage; nothing at 50% or more. § 51-11-7:
   no recovery if ordinary care would have avoided the consequences. Plus Robinson's second prong (§ 2.5).
6. **Who is liable and their coverage.** Practical, not statutory: the property owner's or business's
   liability insurance is usually what pays. **No statute found requiring Georgia businesses or
   homeowners to carry liability insurance** (not researched; DO NOT STATE that coverage is required).
   § 51-12-33(b) means each defendant pays only its share, so identifying every responsible party (owner,
   tenant business, maintenance contractor) matters.
7. **Public property.** Ante litem notice (six months city; twelve months county or State); a possible
   GTCA cap on State claims (PENDING, not to be stated with figures).
8. **Deadline.** § 9-3-33, two years. A claim not filed in time is worth nothing.
