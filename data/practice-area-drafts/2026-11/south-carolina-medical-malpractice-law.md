# South Carolina Medical Malpractice Law: The Notice of Intent, the Expert Affidavit, the Deadlines and the Damages Cap

Last reviewed:

Before you can file a medical malpractice lawsuit in South Carolina, you must file a **Notice of Intent to File Suit** together with a qualified expert's affidavit, serve it on every defendant, and take part in mediation (**S.C. Code § 15-79-125**; **S.C. Code § 15-36-100**). You generally have **three years** to act (**S.C. Code § 15-3-545**), and non-economic damages are capped, at **$596,001 per provider or institution and $1,788,002 in total per claimant** for 2026 (**S.C. Code § 15-32-220**).

If a doctor, nurse or hospital has hurt you or someone you love, the law that decides your claim is stricter and slower than the law for a car crash. This guide sets out each rule in order, so you know what has to happen before a case can even reach a courtroom.

## Key Takeaways

- A South Carolina malpractice case starts with a Notice of Intent to File Suit and an expert's affidavit, filed together, before any lawsuit (S.C. Code § 15-79-125).
- Filing the Notice of Intent tolls all applicable statutes of limitations, and the parties must then mediate within 90 to 120 days of service.
- The general deadline is three years from the treatment or from when the injury was or should have been discovered, and no more than six years from the occurrence (S.C. Code § 15-3-545). A foreign object left in the body and a child's injury follow different rules.
- South Carolina caps non-economic damages: for 2026, $596,001 per health care provider or institution and $1,788,002 in total per claimant (S.C. Code § 15-32-220). Economic damages are not capped by that section.
- The cap does not apply to gross negligence, wilful, wanton or reckless conduct, fraud or misrepresentation, or altered or destroyed medical records.
- A claim against a government hospital follows the Tort Claims Act, with a two-year deadline and its own limits (S.C. Code §§ 15-78-110, 15-78-120).

## What counts as medical malpractice in South Carolina

South Carolina defines medical malpractice as "doing that which the reasonably prudent health care provider or health care institution would not do or not doing that which the reasonably prudent health care provider or health care institution would do in the same or similar circumstances" (S.C. Code § 15-32-210(8)). [VERIFY — attorney: § 15-32-210(8) quoted from primary text, https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t15c032.php, read 2026-11-03; the definition is not a claim in the signed pack.]

In plain terms, a bad outcome is not malpractice on its own. The question is whether the care fell below what a careful provider would have given in the same situation. That is why every case needs an expert, and why South Carolina makes you produce one before you file.

The law reaches institutions as well as individual providers. A "health care institution" includes a hospital, an ambulatory surgical facility and a **nursing home** (S.C. Code § 15-32-210(4)), so the rules below apply to nursing-home malpractice claims too. See our page on [nursing home abuse and neglect claims](/practice-areas/nursing-home-abuse-lawyers/).

## The steps before a lawsuit

South Carolina requires a pre-suit process for every medical malpractice claim. Here it is in order.

| Step | What the law requires | Section |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Notice of Intent and expert affidavit | File a Notice of Intent to File Suit and an expert witness's affidavit at the same time, in a county where venue would be proper | § 15-79-125(A); § 15-36-100 |
| 2. Service | Serve the notice on all named defendants | § 15-79-125(A) |
| 3. Deadline tolled | Filing the notice tolls all applicable statutes of limitations | § 15-79-125(A) |
| 4. Mediation | Mediate within 90 days, and no later than 120 days, of service; a court may extend this by up to 60 days for good cause | § 15-79-125(C) |
| 5. Lawsuit | If mediation does not resolve the claim, you may file the lawsuit | § 15-79-125(E) |

### The expert affidavit

The affidavit must come from a qualified expert and must specify the negligent act or omission (S.C. Code § 15-36-100). The section sets out who qualifies: the expert must be licensed, and either board certified in the specialty the opinion concerns, or have practised or taught in that specialty for at least three of the last five years immediately before giving the opinion.

This is the step that takes time. A lawyer has to get your records, find the right specialist and have that specialist review the care before anything is filed. Graeham C. Gillin, a partner in Roden Law's Charleston office, notes that the expert review is the foundation of the case, not a formality, because the affidavit has to name what went wrong. ### When the lawsuit must be filed after mediation

If mediation ends without a settlement, the lawsuit must be filed within sixty days after the mediator determines that mediation is not viable, that an impasse exists or that mediation should end, or before the statute of limitations runs out, **whichever is later** (S.C. Code § 15-79-125(E)). [VERIFY — attorney: § 15-79-125(E)(1)–(2) from primary text, https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t15c079.php, read 2026-11-03; the signed pack claim says only that the plaintiff may then file.]

## How long you have to file

The deadline depends on the kind of claim.

| Situation | Deadline | Section |
|---|---|---|
| Most malpractice claims | Three years from the treatment, omission or operation, or from when the injury was or reasonably ought to have been discovered; no more than six years from the occurrence | § 15-3-545(A) |
| A foreign object left in the body | Two years from discovery, but never less than three years from the placement; the six-year limit does not apply | § 15-3-545(B) |
| An injured child | The time is not tolled for more than seven years because of the child's age, and in any case not more than one year after the child reaches the age of majority | § 15-3-545(D) |
| A government hospital or clinic | Two years from when the loss was or should have been discovered; three years if a verified claim is filed first | § 15-78-110 |

Filing a Notice of Intent tolls the limitation period (S.C. Code § 15-79-125(A)), but the notice still has to be filed in time, with the expert affidavit, so the clock matters from the start. Read more in our guide to [South Carolina's filing deadlines](/resources/south-carolina-statute-of-limitations/).

## What compensation is available, and the cap

Medical malpractice damages fall into three groups, and South Carolina treats each differently.

**Economic damages** are your financial losses: medical bills, future care, lost wages and lost earning capacity. Section 15-32-220 does not limit them (S.C. Code § 15-32-220(D)(1)).

**Non-economic damages** compensate pain, suffering, mental anguish and loss of enjoyment of life. South Carolina caps them. The statute sets a base of $350,000 per health care provider or institution and $1,050,000 in total for all defendants, per claimant, and adjusts both every year for inflation (S.C. Code § 15-32-220(A)–(C), (F)). For 2026 the figures are:

| Cap | 2026 figure |
|---|---|
| Against a single provider or institution, per claimant | **$596,001** |
| Against all providers and institutions together, per claimant | **$1,788,002** |

Source: South Carolina Revenue and Fiscal Affairs Office memo, February 3, 2026, published in the State Register on February 27, 2026. The figures change every year, so check the current year before relying on them.

The cap does **not** apply if the defendant was grossly negligent, wilful, wanton or reckless and that conduct caused the non-economic damages, if the defendant engaged in fraud or misrepresentation related to the claim, or if the defendant altered or destroyed medical records to avoid a claim (S.C. Code § 15-32-220(E)). This is one reason to request your medical records early.

**Punitive damages** are not limited by § 15-32-220 (S.C. Code § 15-32-220(D)(2)). They have their own rules: they must be proven by clear and convincing evidence (S.C. Code § 15-33-135), and they are generally capped at the greater of three times compensatory damages or $739,245, the 2026 inflation-adjusted figure, with higher limits or no cap for certain conduct (S.C. Code § 15-32-530).

For more on how the limits apply, read our article on [medical malpractice damage caps and filing limits in South Carolina](/blog/medical-malpractice-limits-south-carolina/).

## Special rules that can change a case

**Government hospitals.** A claim against a public hospital or a government-employed doctor follows the South Carolina Tort Claims Act. Recovery is limited to $300,000 per person and $600,000 per occurrence, or $1,200,000 per occurrence where the harm was caused by a licensed physician or dentist employed by a governmental entity, and punitive damages are not available (S.C. Code § 15-78-120).

**Charitable hospitals and nursing homes.** A claim against a 501(c)(3) charitable organization for an employee's negligence is limited to actual damages within the Tort Claims Act limits of $300,000 per person and $600,000 per occurrence (S.C. Code § 33-56-180). That reaches nonprofit hospitals and nursing homes.

**Emergency care.** When care is given in a genuine emergency, involving an immediate threat of death or serious bodily injury, to a patient in an emergency department or an obstetrical or surgical suite, a physician is liable only if grossly negligent (S.C. Code § 15-32-230). The rule applies only while the patient is not medically stable and only to care before discharge from that department or suite, and the section protects physicians, not hospitals. See our page on [emergency room negligence](/medical-malpractice-lawyers/emergency-room-negligence/).

**Informed consent.** South Carolina measures a doctor's duty to disclose risks by what a reasonable medical practitioner of like training would disclose in the same or similar circumstances (*Hook v. Rothstein*, 281 S.C. 541 (Ct. App. 1984)). Read more on [informed consent failure](/medical-malpractice-lawyers/informed-consent-failure/).

**Your own share of fault.** South Carolina's comparative negligence rule applies: you can recover as long as you are less than 51% at fault, and your award is reduced by your share (*Nelson v. Concrete Supply Co.*, 303 S.C. 243 (1991)). See [South Carolina comparative negligence and the 51% bar](/resources/south-carolina-comparative-negligence/).

**When the patient died.** A wrongful death action is brought by the personal representative of the estate (S.C. Code § 15-51-20), and the claim the patient had before death survives to the personal representative (S.C. Code § 15-5-90). See our page on [medical malpractice wrongful death claims](/wrongful-death-lawyers/medical-malpractice-death/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q:** Do I have to file a Notice of Intent before suing for medical malpractice in South Carolina?

**A:** Yes. Before filing a medical malpractice lawsuit, you must file a Notice of Intent to File Suit together with a qualified expert's affidavit, in a county where venue would be proper, and serve it on all named defendants (S.C. Code § 15-79-125). The parties must then mediate before the lawsuit is filed.

**Q:** What does the expert affidavit have to say?

**A:** It must come from a qualified expert and specify the negligent act or omission (S.C. Code § 15-36-100). The expert must be licensed and either board certified in the specialty or have practised or taught in it for at least three of the last five years before giving the opinion.

**Q:** How long do I have to file a medical malpractice claim in South Carolina?

**A:** Generally three years from the treatment, or from when the injury was or reasonably should have been discovered, and no more than six years from the occurrence (S.C. Code § 15-3-545). A foreign object left in the body and an injury to a child follow different rules, and a claim against a government hospital generally has two years (S.C. Code § 15-78-110).

**Q:** Does South Carolina cap medical malpractice damages?

**A:** Yes, for non-economic damages. For 2026 the cap is $596,001 per health care provider or institution and $1,788,002 in total per claimant, adjusted every year for inflation (S.C. Code § 15-32-220). Economic damages such as medical bills and lost wages are not limited by that section, and the cap does not apply to gross negligence, wilful, wanton or reckless conduct, fraud or misrepresentation, or altered or destroyed records.

**Q:** Is mediation required in a South Carolina malpractice case?

**A:** Yes. After the Notice of Intent is served, the parties must mediate within 90 days and no later than 120 days of service, unless a court extends that by up to 60 days for good cause (S.C. Code § 15-79-125(C)). If mediation does not resolve the claim, you may file the lawsuit.

**Q:** Can I sue a public hospital for malpractice in South Carolina?

**A:** Yes, under the South Carolina Tort Claims Act. The deadline is generally two years (S.C. Code § 15-78-110), recovery is limited to $300,000 per person and $600,000 per occurrence, or $1,200,000 per occurrence for harm caused by a government-employed physician or dentist, and punitive damages are not available (S.C. Code § 15-78-120).
