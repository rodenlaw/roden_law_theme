# How Much Does Georgia Workers' Comp Pay? Weekly Checks, Body-Part Ratings and the Deadlines That Protect Them

Last reviewed:

If a work injury keeps you out of work for more than seven days, Georgia workers' compensation pays you **two-thirds of your average weekly wage**, up to a weekly maximum set by statute (**O.C.G.A. § 34-9-261**). For injuries on or after July 1, 2023, the State Board of Workers' Compensation states that maximum as **$800 a week**. [VERIFY — attorney: $800 maximum and its July 1, 2023 effective date from the State Board's Workers' Compensation Law FAQ, https://sbwc.georgia.gov/frequently-asked-questions/workers-compensation-law-faqs, read 2026-11-03; the GA pack claim for § 34-9-261 says to confirm the current figure before publishing any number, and no 2024–2026 amendment has been checked.]

When you are hurt on the job, the first question is usually the simplest one: how much will I be paid while I cannot work? This guide answers it for Georgia, one benefit at a time, and shows you the two deadlines that can cost you the check entirely.

## Key Takeaways

- Georgia's weekly income benefit for total disability is two-thirds of your average weekly wage, up to the statutory maximum (O.C.G.A. § 34-9-261).
- You must miss more than seven days of work before weekly benefits are owed.
- If you go back to work at lower pay because of the injury, a reduced-earnings benefit may make up part of the difference (O.C.G.A. § 34-9-262).
- A permanent impairment to a body part listed in the statute is paid for a set number of weeks, such as 225 weeks for an arm or a leg (O.C.G.A. § 34-9-263).
- Workers' comp does not pay for pain and suffering, and you generally cannot sue your employer, but you may have a separate claim against someone else who caused the injury (O.C.G.A. § 34-9-11).
- Report the injury to your employer within 30 days (O.C.G.A. § 34-9-80) and file your claim with the State Board in time (O.C.G.A. § 34-9-82), or you can lose these benefits.

## How much is the weekly check?

The weekly benefit while you cannot work at all is called **temporary total disability (TTD)**. It is two-thirds of your average weekly wage before the injury, but never more than the statutory maximum (O.C.G.A. § 34-9-261).

| Your average weekly wage | Two-thirds | What you are paid (at an $800 maximum) |
|---|---|---|
| $600 | $400 | $400 |
| $900 | $600 | $600 |
| $1,200 | $800 | $800 |
| $1,500 | $1,000 | $800 |

[VERIFY — attorney: the table's last column depends on the $800 figure above; recompute if the maximum differs.]

The arithmetic matters most for higher earners. Once two-thirds of your wage passes the maximum, every extra dollar you earned before the injury makes no difference to the check.

Your average weekly wage is based on what you earned before the injury. If your pay varied, or you had a second job, the figure the insurer uses is worth checking line by line.

### When the first check comes

Weekly benefits are owed only if you are unable to work for more than seven days. The State Board says the first check should be mailed within 21 days after the first day you missed work, and that if you miss more than 21 consecutive days, you are also paid for the first week. [VERIFY — attorney: the seven-day waiting period, the 21-day first payment and the first-week rule are from the State Board's Workers' Compensation Law FAQ (URL above), read 2026-11-03; trace to O.C.G.A. § 34-9-220 and § 34-9-221 before publishing, neither of which is in the GA pack or on the Verified table.]

## What if you go back to work at lower pay?

If your doctor releases you to light duty and you can only earn less than you did before, Georgia pays a reduced benefit called **temporary partial disability (TPD)** (O.C.G.A. § 34-9-262). It is based on the difference between your old and new earnings. The State Board states that it cannot exceed $533 a week for injuries on or after July 1, 2023, and that it is paid for no more than 350 weeks from the date of injury. [VERIFY — attorney: $533, its effective date and the 350-week limit from the State Board's Workers' Compensation Law FAQ (URL above), read 2026-11-03; § 34-9-262 is not in the GA pack or on the Verified table.]

Keep every pay stub from the lighter job. The reduced benefit is only as accurate as the earnings records behind it.

## What is a body-part rating worth?

When your treatment ends and you are left with a lasting impairment, the authorized treating doctor gives you an impairment rating. Georgia then pays **permanent partial disability (PPD)** benefits for a set number of weeks, depending on the body part (O.C.G.A. § 34-9-263).

| Body part (total loss or loss of use) | Weeks under the schedule |
|---|---|
| Arm | 225 |
| Leg | 225 |
| Hand | 160 |
| Eye | 150 |
| Foot | 135 |

Source: O.C.G.A. § 34-9-263(c), read in the Roden statute pass of 2026-10-24 against a publisher copy current to 2024.

A partial loss is paid in proportion to the rating. As an example, a 10% rating to the arm is 10% of 225 weeks, or 22.5 weeks of benefits. [VERIFY — attorney: confirm that PPD is paid at the claimant's TTD rate (rating × scheduled weeks × weekly rate) under § 34-9-263(b), and that the doctor rates under the AMA Guides, fifth edition, as the State Board's FAQ states; neither point is in the GA pack.]

Two rules from the same section affect when the money arrives. PPD is payable whether or not the injury costs you any earnings, and it is not paid for weeks in which you are receiving TTD or TPD benefits (O.C.G.A. § 34-9-263(b)).

## What workers' comp does not pay

Georgia workers' compensation is your **exclusive remedy against your employer** (O.C.G.A. § 34-9-11). That means you generally cannot sue your employer for the injury, and comp does not pay for pain and suffering or award punitive damages.

It does not stop you from suing someone else. If a driver from another company hit your work vehicle, or a defective machine caused the injury, you may have a separate injury claim against that third party, where pain and suffering is recoverable. The employer or its insurer may have a lien on that recovery for benefits it paid, but only if you have been fully and completely compensated (O.C.G.A. § 34-9-11.1(b)). ## The two deadlines that protect your check

Every benefit on this page can be lost to a missed deadline.

| Step | Deadline | Section |
|---|---|---|
| Tell your employer about the injury | Within 30 days | O.C.G.A. § 34-9-80 |
| File your claim (Form WC-14) with the State Board | One year from the injury; or one year from the last remedial treatment your employer furnished; or two years from the last weekly benefit payment | O.C.G.A. § 34-9-82 |

There is no two-years-from-injury rule for a workers' compensation claim. The two-year deadline you may have heard about is the one for personal injury lawsuits (O.C.G.A. § 9-3-33; see our guide to [Georgia's personal injury filing deadline](/resources/georgia-statute-of-limitations/)), not for comp. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q:** How much does Georgia workers' comp pay per week?

**A:** Two-thirds of your average weekly wage, up to the weekly maximum set by O.C.G.A. § 34-9-261. The State Board of Workers' Compensation states that maximum as $800 a week for injuries on or after July 1, 2023. [VERIFY — attorney: same figure as the opening section.]

**Q:** Is there a waiting period before Georgia workers' comp pays?

**A:** Yes. Weekly benefits are owed only if you are unable to work for more than seven days, and if you miss more than 21 consecutive days you are also paid for the first week. [VERIFY — attorney: same source and statute trace as "When the first check comes".]

**Q:** Do I get paid if I go back to work at a lower-paying job?

**A:** You may. Temporary partial disability benefits under O.C.G.A. § 34-9-262 make up part of the difference between what you earned before the injury and what you earn now. [VERIFY — attorney: § 34-9-262 not in the GA pack.]

**Q:** How is a permanent impairment paid in Georgia?

**A:** The authorized treating doctor gives an impairment rating, and Georgia pays for a set number of weeks depending on the body part, for example 225 weeks for an arm or a leg and 160 weeks for a hand (O.C.G.A. § 34-9-263). A partial loss is paid in proportion to the rating.

**Q:** Can I get pain and suffering from Georgia workers' comp?

**A:** No. Workers' compensation is the exclusive remedy against your employer and does not pay for pain and suffering (O.C.G.A. § 34-9-11). If someone other than your employer caused the injury, you may have a separate claim against them that can include it.

**Q:** How long do I have to file a Georgia workers' comp claim?

**A:** Report the injury to your employer within 30 days (O.C.G.A. § 34-9-80). File your claim with the State Board within one year of the injury, one year from the last remedial treatment your employer furnished, or two years from the last weekly benefit payment (O.C.G.A. § 34-9-82).
