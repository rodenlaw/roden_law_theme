After a serious accident, the bills you can see — medical expenses, vehicle repairs, lost paychecks — are only part of what the injury costs you. The sleepless nights, the anxiety about getting behind the wheel again, the hobbies you can no longer enjoy — these losses are just as real, and the law recognizes them as pain and suffering damages.

If you've been injured in Georgia or South Carolina, understanding how pain and suffering is valued, calculated, and proven can mean the difference between a lowball insurance offer and the full compensation you deserve. This guide explains how courts and insurers approach pain and suffering in both states.

What Is Pain and Suffering in a Personal Injury Case?

Pain and suffering is a legal term that refers to the physical pain and emotional distress a person endures as a result of an injury caused by someone else's negligence. Unlike economic damages such as medical bills and lost wages, pain and suffering falls into the category of non-economic damages — also called general damages — because there is no receipt or invoice that proves their exact dollar value.

Despite being harder to quantify, pain and suffering damages are often the largest component of a personal injury settlement or verdict. For an overview of how non-economic damages fit into the broader landscape of injury compensation, see Cornell Law Institute's legal definition of damages.

Types of Pain and Suffering Damages

Courts in Georgia and South Carolina recognize two broad categories of pain and suffering:

Physical Pain and Suffering

This covers the bodily pain you have experienced and are expected to experience in the future as a result of your injuries. Examples include:

  • Acute pain from broken bones, surgeries, and medical procedures
  • Chronic pain that persists long after the initial injury has healed
  • Physical limitations — difficulty walking, standing, lifting, or performing daily tasks
  • Disfigurement and scarring — permanent physical changes that affect your appearance and self-image
  • Loss of bodily function — paralysis, reduced mobility, loss of a limb, or sensory impairment

Emotional and Mental Pain and Suffering

Injuries do not just affect the body. The psychological impact of a serious accident can be equally devastating:

  • Anxiety and depression triggered by the accident or its aftermath
  • Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) — flashbacks, nightmares, and hypervigilance
  • Fear and phobias — fear of driving, fear of medical procedures, or generalized anxiety
  • Loss of enjoyment of life — the inability to participate in hobbies, sports, social activities, and family events you enjoyed before the injury
  • Loss of consortium — the strain on your relationship with your spouse, including loss of companionship, affection, and intimacy
  • Insomnia and sleep disturbances caused by pain or psychological distress
  • Humiliation and embarrassment — particularly in cases involving disfigurement or loss of bodily function

How Pain and Suffering Is Calculated

Neither Georgia nor South Carolina law sets a formula for calculating pain and suffering. Instead, insurance adjusters, attorneys, and juries use several accepted methods to arrive at a number that reflects the severity of what you have endured.

The Multiplier Method

The most widely used approach. Your total economic damages (medical bills, lost wages, property damage, and other out-of-pocket costs) are multiplied by a factor — typically between 1.5 and 5 — to estimate the value of your non-economic losses.

The multiplier increases based on injury severity:

  • 1.5 – 2× — minor injuries with full recovery (soft tissue, minor whiplash)
  • 2 – 3× — moderate injuries requiring extended treatment (herniated discs, fractures)
  • 3 – 4× — serious injuries with lasting effects (multiple surgeries, partial disability)
  • 4 – 5× or higher — catastrophic or permanent injuries (traumatic brain injuries, spinal cord injuries, amputation)

Example: If your economic damages total $80,000 and a multiplier of 3 is applied, your pain and suffering would be valued at $240,000, bringing the total claim to $320,000.

The Per Diem Method

This approach assigns a daily dollar amount to your pain and suffering and multiplies it by the number of days you are expected to be affected. The daily rate is often tied to your actual daily earnings, on the theory that your suffering each day is worth at least as much as your daily wage.

Example: If you earn $200 per day and your recovery period is 365 days, the per diem calculation yields $73,000 in pain and suffering damages.

The per diem method works best for injuries with a defined recovery timeline. For permanent injuries, the multiplier method is generally more appropriate because it accounts for lifelong impact without requiring a specific end date.

Jury Discretion

If your case goes to trial, the jury has broad discretion to award whatever amount it believes fairly compensates you for your pain and suffering. Jurors consider the evidence, the severity of injuries, the credibility of testimony, and their own sense of fairness. This is why presenting a compelling, well-documented case is critical.

Factors That Affect the Value of Pain and Suffering

Every case is different, but certain factors consistently increase or decrease the value of pain and suffering damages:

Factors That Increase Value

  • Severity of injuries — catastrophic injuries such as severe burns, traumatic brain injuries, or spinal cord damage command higher awards
  • Permanence — injuries that cause lasting disability, chronic pain, or permanent scarring are valued significantly higher than temporary injuries
  • Length of recovery — longer treatment and rehabilitation periods reflect greater suffering
  • Impact on daily life — if you can no longer work, exercise, care for your children, or live independently, your claim increases
  • Clear liability — when fault is obvious (e.g., a drunk driver rear-ended you), insurers are more likely to offer fair compensation
  • Documented mental health treatment — therapy records, psychiatric evaluations, and prescriptions for anxiety or depression corroborate emotional suffering
  • Consistent medical treatment — following your doctor's orders and attending all appointments demonstrates that your injuries are serious

Factors That Decrease Value

  • Gaps in medical treatment — if you waited weeks to see a doctor or missed follow-up appointments, the insurer will argue your injuries were not serious
  • Pre-existing conditions — insurers will try to attribute your pain to a condition that existed before the accident, though Georgia and South Carolina both follow the eggshell plaintiff rule (you take the victim as you find them)
  • Inconsistent statements — contradictions between what you told the doctor, the police, and the insurance company can undermine your credibility
  • Social media activity — photos or posts showing physical activity inconsistent with your claimed injuries can significantly damage your case
  • Shared fault — if you were partially responsible for the accident, your award will be reduced (see comparative fault below)

Pain and Suffering Caps in Georgia and South Carolina

One of the most common questions injury victims ask is whether there is a limit on what they can recover for pain and suffering. The answer depends on the type of case:

Georgia

No cap on pain and suffering in most personal injury cases. Georgia does not limit non-economic damages in car accidents, truck accidents, premises liability, or other general negligence claims. However, Georgia previously had a cap on non-economic damages in medical malpractice cases, which was struck down as unconstitutional by the Georgia Supreme Court in Atlanta Oculoplastic Surgery, P.C. v. Nestlehutt (2010). As a result, there is currently no cap on pain and suffering in any personal injury case in Georgia.

South Carolina

No general cap on pain and suffering in most personal injury cases. South Carolina does not limit non-economic damages in car accident, truck accident, or premises liability cases. However, South Carolina does cap non-economic damages in medical malpractice cases at $350,000 per defendant and $1.05 million total regardless of the number of defendants (S.C. Code § 15-32-220).

Outside of medical malpractice, juries in both states have the freedom to award whatever amount they believe is fair based on the evidence presented.

How Comparative Fault Reduces Your Award

If you share some responsibility for the accident, your total recovery — including pain and suffering — will be reduced under each state's modified comparative fault rules:

  • Georgia (O.C.G.A. § 51-12-33): You can recover as long as you are less than 50% at fault. Your award is reduced by your percentage of fault. At 50% or more, you recover nothing.
  • South Carolina: You can recover as long as you are less than 51% at fault. Your award is reduced by your percentage of fault. At 51% or more, you recover nothing.

Example: A jury awards you $200,000 in total damages (including pain and suffering) but finds you 25% at fault. Your award is reduced to $150,000.

Common Cases That Involve Pain and Suffering Claims

Pain and suffering damages are available in virtually every personal injury case where negligence caused physical or emotional harm. The types of cases that most frequently produce significant pain and suffering awards include:

  • Car accidents — the most common source of personal injury claims, ranging from minor whiplash to catastrophic injuries
  • Truck accidents — collisions with commercial vehicles often cause severe, life-altering injuries due to the size disparity
  • Motorcycle accidents — riders lack the protection of an enclosed vehicle, resulting in higher rates of serious injury
  • Pedestrian accidents — pedestrians struck by vehicles suffer some of the most severe injuries
  • Slip and fall injuries — falls can cause broken bones, head injuries, and chronic back pain, especially for older adults
  • Medical malpractice — surgical errors, misdiagnosis, and medication mistakes can cause prolonged suffering
  • Wrongful death — surviving family members can recover for their own pain and suffering from the loss of a loved one
  • Dog bites — attacks often cause scarring, disfigurement, and lasting psychological trauma
  • Nursing home abuse — elderly victims of neglect or abuse suffer significant physical and emotional harm
  • Premises liability — injuries on unsafe property due to a property owner's negligence
  • Product liability — defective products that cause injury, including burns, lacerations, or toxic exposure

How to Prove Pain and Suffering

Because pain and suffering is subjective, the strength of your claim depends on the quality of your evidence. Insurance companies routinely challenge non-economic damages as exaggerated or unrelated to the accident. The following types of evidence help establish and quantify your suffering:

  • Medical records — documentation of your injuries, treatment plans, prescriptions, and prognosis from your treating physicians
  • Mental health records — therapy notes, psychiatric evaluations, and diagnoses of PTSD, anxiety, or depression linked to the accident
  • A pain journal — a daily or weekly record of your pain levels, limitations, emotional state, and how the injury affects your routine
  • Photographs and video — images of your injuries at various stages of healing, your accident scene, and any assistive devices you use
  • Testimony from family and friends — statements from people who have observed changes in your personality, mood, physical abilities, and daily life since the accident
  • Expert testimony — medical experts, psychologists, or life-care planners who can explain the extent of your suffering and its projected duration to a jury

How a Personal Injury Lawyer Maximizes Pain and Suffering Damages

Insurance companies have one goal: pay as little as possible. They use sophisticated software to generate lowball offers, and they know that unrepresented claimants are more likely to accept those offers. An experienced personal injury attorney fights back by:

  • Building a comprehensive damages case — gathering medical records, expert opinions, and documentary evidence that captures the full scope of your suffering
  • Selecting the right valuation method — applying the multiplier or per diem approach (or both) depending on which produces the most accurate and persuasive result for your injuries
  • Countering insurance tactics — challenging attempts to blame pre-existing conditions, gaps in treatment, or shared fault to reduce your claim
  • Preparing for trial — insurers offer more when they know your attorney is willing and prepared to take the case before a jury
