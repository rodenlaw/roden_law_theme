A demand letter is often the first real step toward recovering compensation after an injury. Before a lawsuit is ever filed, before a jury is ever seated, the demand letter sets the tone for the entire negotiation. It tells the insurance company what happened, what you lost, and what you expect them to pay. According to the American Bar Association, most personal injury claims settle before trial, and the demand letter is the document that starts that settlement conversation.

If you were hurt in a car accident, a slip-and-fall, or any other incident caused by someone else's negligence, understanding how the demand letter works under Georgia and South Carolina law gives you a clearer picture of the process ahead.

What Is a Personal Injury Demand Letter?

A personal injury demand letter is a formal written document sent from the injured party (or their attorney) to the at-fault party's insurance company. It lays out the facts of the incident, identifies the legal basis for the claim, summarizes the injuries and losses, and states a specific dollar amount the injured party will accept to resolve the claim without litigation.

The demand letter is not a lawsuit. It is a pre-suit communication that serves several purposes at once. It puts the insurance company on notice that you are serious about your claim. It establishes a documented record of your damages. And it gives the insurer a defined window to respond before you file suit in court.

In personal injury cases involving truck accidents, motorcycle crashes, or pedestrian collisions, the demand letter often represents months of preparation. Your attorney gathers medical records, calculates lost income, obtains expert opinions, and builds a comprehensive picture of your damages before putting pen to paper.

What Should a Demand Letter Include?

A strong demand letter reads as a persuasive legal argument, not a fill-in-the-blank form. It is a tailored legal argument built around the specific facts of your case. Here is what every effective demand letter should contain.

A Clear Narrative of What Happened

The letter opens with a factual account of the incident. This section describes when and where the accident occurred, who was involved, and how the at-fault party caused or contributed to the accident. It reads like a short story grounded in evidence: police reports, witness statements, and surveillance footage all support the narrative. The goal is to make the adjuster understand exactly what happened and why their insured is liable.

The Liability Argument

This is where the letter transitions from storytelling to legal analysis. Your attorney identifies the specific duty of care the defendant owed you, how they breached that duty, and how that breach directly caused your injuries. In a dog bite case, for example, the liability argument might cite the owner's knowledge of the dog's aggressive history. In a medical malpractice claim, it would detail how the provider deviated from the accepted standard of care.

A Detailed Damages Itemization

The damages section is the backbone of the demand letter. It lists every category of loss with supporting documentation. Economic damages include hospital bills, surgical costs, physical therapy charges, prescription expenses, lost wages, diminished earning capacity, and anticipated future medical needs. Non-economic damages cover pain and suffering, emotional distress, loss of enjoyment of life, and the impact of the injury on daily activities and personal relationships.

Every dollar figure should be backed by a medical bill, a pay stub, an expert report, or another verifiable document. Vague or unsupported numbers invite the insurer to discount your entire claim.

A Summary of Supporting Evidence

The demand letter references (and typically attaches) the key evidence that proves both liability and damages. This may include the accident report, photographs of the scene and injuries, medical records and imaging studies, employment records showing lost income, and expert opinions from treating physicians or accident reconstruction specialists.

The Demand Amount

The letter concludes with a specific dollar amount that the injured party will accept to resolve the claim. This number is not pulled out of thin air. It reflects the total documented damages plus a reasonable valuation of pain and suffering, informed by jury verdicts and settlements in comparable cases within the jurisdiction.

A Response Deadline

Every demand letter includes a deadline for the insurance company to respond, typically 30 days. The deadline creates urgency and makes clear that if the insurer fails to respond or refuses to negotiate in good faith, the next step is filing a lawsuit.

When to Send a Demand Letter — Timing Matters

One of the most common mistakes in personal injury cases is sending the demand letter too early. If you send the demand before you have reached maximum medical improvement (MMI), you risk undervaluing your claim because you do not yet know the full extent of your injuries or the total cost of your treatment.

MMI is the point at which your treating physician determines that your condition has stabilized and is unlikely to improve further with additional treatment. For minor soft-tissue injuries from a fender-bender, MMI might come within a few months. For serious injuries like traumatic brain injuries or spinal cord damage, it could take a year or longer.

At the same time, you cannot wait indefinitely. Georgia imposes a two-year statute of limitations under O.C.G.A. § 9-3-33, while South Carolina allows three years under S.C. Code § 15-3-530. Your demand letter should go out early enough to allow meaningful negotiation time before those filing deadlines arrive. Most attorneys aim to send the demand at least four to six months before the statute of limitations expires.

The Demand Letter Process in Georgia

Georgia does not require you to send a demand letter before filing a personal injury lawsuit. However, sending one is standard practice because it often leads to faster resolution and avoids the cost and delay of litigation.

Pre-Suit Requirements and Strategic Considerations

While there is no blanket pre-suit demand requirement for private negligence claims in Georgia, certain categories of claims do have mandatory notice provisions. If you are bringing a claim against a Georgia city, you must provide written ante litem notice within six months of the incident under O.C.G.A. § 36-33-5. A claim against a county must be presented within 12 months under O.C.G.A. § 36-11-1. For claims against the state itself, ante litem notice must be provided under O.C.G.A. § 50-21-26. Failing to send this notice within the required timeframe bars your claim entirely, regardless of how strong the evidence is.

Bad Faith Demand Under O.C.G.A. § 33-4-6

Georgia has a powerful tool that experienced personal injury attorneys use to put additional pressure on insurance companies. Under O.C.G.A. § 33-4-6, if an insurer refuses to pay a covered claim in bad faith, the insured can recover a penalty of up to 50% of the liability plus reasonable attorney's fees. To trigger bad faith liability, you must send a written demand giving the insurer 60 days to pay. If the insurer fails to pay within that window and cannot show a reasonable basis for its refusal, the penalty applies.

This statute makes the demand letter more than a negotiation tool in Georgia. It becomes a legal mechanism that can significantly increase the insurer's exposure. Insurers take demands structured under O.C.G.A. § 33-4-6 seriously because the financial consequences of ignoring them are steep.

Georgia's Comparative Fault Considerations

When drafting a demand letter for a Georgia claim, your attorney must address the state's modified comparative fault rule under O.C.G.A. § 51-12-33. Georgia bars recovery if you are 50% or more at fault for the accident. Insurance adjusters routinely argue that the injured party bears partial blame in order to reduce or eliminate the payout. A well-crafted demand letter anticipates this defense and addresses it head-on with evidence that places the majority of fault on the defendant.

The Demand Letter Process in South Carolina

Like Georgia, South Carolina does not require a pre-suit demand letter for standard personal injury claims. But the demand letter remains an essential part of the process for the same practical reasons: it opens negotiations, documents your claim, and often leads to settlement without the need for a trial.

Statute of Limitations Under S.C. Code § 15-3-530

South Carolina gives injured parties three years from the date of injury to file a personal injury lawsuit under S.C. Code § 15-3-530. While the extra year compared to Georgia provides more breathing room, it should not be treated as an invitation to delay. Evidence degrades over time, witnesses become harder to locate, and insurance companies view late claims with more skepticism. The demand letter should still go out well before the three-year deadline to preserve maximum negotiating leverage.

No Mandatory Pre-Suit Demand for Bad Faith

Unlike Georgia's statutory bad faith framework under O.C.G.A. § 33-4-6, South Carolina does not have a specific pre-suit demand requirement to trigger bad faith penalties against insurers. Bad faith claims against insurance companies in South Carolina are governed by common law and the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. An insurer that unreasonably refuses to pay a valid claim can face liability for bad faith, but the procedural path is different. There is no 60-day demand window comparable to Georgia's statute.

That said, sending a clear, well-documented demand letter still matters in South Carolina. It creates a paper trail that demonstrates the insurer had full knowledge of the claim and chose not to settle, which strengthens any subsequent bad faith argument at trial.

South Carolina's Comparative Fault Considerations

South Carolina applies a modified comparative fault standard that bars recovery if you are 51% or more at fault. This is slightly more favorable to plaintiffs than Georgia's 50% threshold. In the demand letter, your attorney will present the evidence in a way that minimizes any argument the insurer might make about your contributory negligence, whether the case involves a wrongful death claim or a survivable injury.

What Happens After You Send the Demand Letter

Once the demand letter is delivered, the insurance company has a limited window to respond. What happens next generally falls into one of four categories.

The Insurer Accepts the Demand

This is the best-case scenario, though it happens rarely on the first demand. If the insurer agrees to pay the full amount requested, the parties execute a settlement agreement and release, and the injured party receives payment. In straightforward cases with clear liability and well-documented damages, a full acceptance is possible, particularly when the demand amount falls within the policy limits.

The Insurer Makes a Counteroffer

This is the most common response. The insurer reviews the demand, disputes certain damage calculations or liability percentages, and comes back with a lower number. This is where negotiation begins in earnest. Your attorney evaluates the counteroffer against the strengths and weaknesses of the case and responds with a revised demand or a rebuttal explaining why the original amount is justified. Several rounds of back-and-forth may follow before the parties reach an agreement.

The Insurer Rejects the Demand

A flat rejection signals that the insurer either disputes liability entirely or believes the claim is worth significantly less than what you demanded. In some cases, the insurer may deny the claim outright, arguing that their insured was not at fault. When this happens, your attorney may attempt further negotiation or proceed directly to filing a lawsuit.

The Insurer Ignores the Demand

If the insurance company fails to respond within the stated deadline, your attorney will typically follow up with a final notice before filing suit. In Georgia, ignoring a properly structured demand under O.C.G.A. § 33-4-6 can expose the insurer to the statutory bad faith penalty, which gives adjusters a strong incentive to at least acknowledge the demand and engage in the process.

Common Mistakes That Weaken a Demand Letter

Not all demand letters are created equal. A poorly drafted letter can undermine an otherwise strong claim. Here are the mistakes that do the most damage.

Sending the demand before reaching MMI. If you have not finished treatment, you are guessing at your total damages. Insurance companies will use the incomplete picture against you.

Inflating the demand amount without justification. Asking for an unreasonable sum without documentation to support it signals to the adjuster that you are not serious. It does not set the stage for a higher settlement; it invites the insurer to dismiss the entire demand.

Failing to address comparative fault. In both Georgia and South Carolina, the insurer will look for any evidence that you contributed to the accident. A demand letter that ignores this issue leaves the door open for the adjuster to argue a high percentage of fault against you.

Using generic or template language. Adjusters review thousands of demand letters. They can tell immediately when a letter is a boilerplate form with a few names and dates filled in. A generic letter suggests a lack of preparation and invites a lowball counteroffer.

Omitting key evidence. If you reference a medical record or an expert opinion but do not attach it, the adjuster has no obligation to track it down. Every claim in the demand letter must be supported by attached documentation.

Missing the statute of limitations. This is the most devastating mistake. If you send the demand too late and the statute expires before you can file suit, the insurer has zero incentive to pay anything. You lose all leverage.

Why You Need a Lawyer to Write Your Demand Letter

You can technically write and send a demand letter yourself. Nothing in Georgia or South Carolina law prevents it. But doing so almost always results in a lower settlement or an outright denial.

Insurance adjusters are trained negotiators working within institutional systems designed to minimize payouts. When they receive a demand letter from an unrepresented claimant, they know that the person on the other side is unlikely to file a lawsuit if the offer is low. The adjuster's leverage increases dramatically when there is no attorney involved.

An experienced personal injury attorney brings several advantages to the demand letter process. They know how to calculate the full value of a claim, including future medical costs and non-economic damages that injured people often overlook. They understand how Georgia's bad faith statute and South Carolina's common law bad faith standards affect strategy. They know which evidence will carry the most weight with a particular insurance company. And they have the credibility to follow through on the implicit threat behind every demand letter: if you do not settle this fairly, we will take it to trial.

Whether your case involves a rear-end collision or a catastrophic brain injury, the demand letter is too important to leave to chance. It is the document that shapes the entire trajectory of your claim.
