After a car accident, truck crash, or any personal injury in South Carolina, you have a legal deadline to file your lawsuit. Miss it, and your claim is permanently barred — no matter how serious your injuries or how clear the other driver's fault. Here's what you need to know about South Carolina's statute of limitations.

The Basic Rule: 3 Years

Under S.C. Code § 15-3-530, you have 3 years from the date of your injury to file a personal injury lawsuit in South Carolina. This applies to:

  • Car accidents
  • Truck accidents
  • Motorcycle crashes
  • Pedestrian and bicycle accidents
  • Slip and fall injuries
  • Dog bites
  • Medical malpractice (with specific rules)
  • Any other negligence-based injury claim

Why Waiting Hurts You (Even Within the Deadline)

The statute of limitations is the last day you can file — not the ideal day. Every month you wait costs you:

Evidence Degrades

  • Surveillance footage is overwritten, often within days
  • Traffic camera footage, if recorded at all, is not kept long
  • Dashcam and engine data have no federal retention rule; truck ELD logs need be kept only six months (49 C.F.R. § 395.8(k))
  • Vehicle damage is repaired or the car is scrapped
  • Road conditions change (potholes fixed, signs added, construction completed)

Witnesses Forget

  • Memories fade, and details become less reliable over time
  • Witnesses move, change phone numbers, or become unreachable
  • Police officers who responded may transfer or retire

Insurance Companies Notice

  • Insurers may read a long delay as a sign the claim is minor
  • Gaps between the accident and medical treatment suggest injuries aren't severe
  • Insurers know an unrepresented claimant is less likely to sue, and offers can reflect it

The Discovery Rule

Under S.C. Code § 15-3-535, the three years run from when the injured person knew or reasonably should have known they had a claim. This "discovery rule" matters most for:

  • Medical malpractice (delayed diagnosis)
  • Toxic exposure with latent symptoms
  • Defective products causing gradual harm

In a car accident the rule usually changes nothing: you know you were hurt on the day of the crash — and an injury that later worsens does not restart the clock (Dean v. Ruscon Corp., 1996).

Tolling: When the Clock Pauses

The statute of limitations may be "tolled" (paused) in specific circumstances:

  • Minor plaintiff: If under 18, the deadline is generally the 19th birthday, or three years from the injury if later (S.C. Code § 15-3-40)
  • Defendant absent from state: If the at-fault party moves out of state, or is away a year or more, that time may not count (S.C. Code § 15-3-30)
  • Mental incapacity: If legally incompetent when the claim arises, the clock pauses for at most five years and at most one year after it ends (S.C. Code §§ 15-3-40, 15-3-50)

What "Filing" Means

The statute requires that your lawsuit be filed with the court — not that settlement negotiations be ongoing, not that you've hired an attorney, not that you've sent a demand letter. The Summons and Complaint must be filed with the Clerk of Court before the 3-year anniversary of your injury, and the defendant must be served within 120 days after filing (S.C. Code § 15-3-20(B)).
