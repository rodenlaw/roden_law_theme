Savannah sits at the convergence of two major interstate highways and is served by several high-traffic arterials that carry far more vehicles than they were designed to handle. The result is a network of roads where serious and fatal crashes occur with alarming regularity. According to the Georgia Governor's Office of Highway Safety, Chatham County ranks among the state's top counties for traffic fatalities, and driver negligence on Savannah's most dangerous highways is the leading cause. Interstate 16, Interstate 95, Abercorn Street, DeRenne Avenue, and I-516 (Lynes Parkway) each present distinct dangers — from high-speed commercial truck collisions to congestion-fueled rear-end pileups. If you have been injured on one of these dangerous corridors, understanding the specific risks and your legal rights is essential to recovering fair compensation.

Why Savannah's Highways Are So Dangerous

Savannah's highway danger stems from a combination of geography, growth, and traffic patterns that create a perfect storm of crash risk.

Port traffic: The Port of Savannah is the fourth-busiest container port in the United States, and the commercial truck traffic it generates dominates Savannah's highways. I-16, I-95, and I-516 all carry heavy volumes of tractor-trailers traveling to and from the port, and the size and weight of these vehicles make crashes far more likely to produce catastrophic injuries or death.

Tourism: Savannah attracts more than 14 million visitors annually, flooding roads with unfamiliar drivers navigating historic district streets, suburban corridors, and highway interchanges they have never encountered before.

Outdated infrastructure: Many of Savannah's busiest roads were designed decades ago for much lower traffic volumes. Abercorn Street, DeRenne Avenue, and key interstate interchanges operate at or above capacity during peak hours, creating congestion that leads to aggressive driving and chain-reaction collisions.

Interstate 16: The "Devil's Highway"

Interstate 16 stretches 167 miles from Savannah to Macon, and the Savannah-area segment of this highway has earned the grim nickname "Devil's Highway" for its history of fatal collisions. The final stretch of I-16 as it approaches Savannah carries a dense mix of commuter traffic, long-haul freight trucks, and vehicles connecting to I-95, and the resulting congestion and speed differentials create extremely dangerous conditions.

Key danger zones on I-16:

  • The I-16/Chatham Parkway interchange handles more than 25,000 vehicles daily and is one of the most collision-prone locations in Chatham County. Short merge ramps force vehicles to enter the highway at dangerous speed differentials.
  • The I-16/I-95 junction is a major bottleneck where long-distance interstate traffic merges with local commuter traffic, producing frequent rear-end collisions and multi-vehicle pileups.
  • The I-16/I-516 split near downtown Savannah confuses unfamiliar drivers and produces sideswipe crashes as vehicles attempt last-second lane changes.

Truck accidents on I-16 are particularly devastating. A fully loaded tractor-trailer traveling at highway speed can weigh 80,000 pounds, and the force of a collision between a commercial truck and a passenger vehicle at 65+ mph frequently produces traumatic brain injuries, spinal cord injuries, and fatalities. If a truck driver's fatigue, distraction, or impairment caused your crash on I-16, you may have claims against both the driver and the trucking company.

Interstate 95 Through Chatham County

Interstate 95 is the most heavily traveled north-south highway on the East Coast, and it runs directly through the Savannah metropolitan area. Federal crash data bears that out: across 2022–2024, I-95 recorded more fatal crashes than any US interstate except I-10 — 924 fatal crashes and 1,020 deaths nationally, second of 222 interstate designations (NHTSA Fatality Analysis Reporting System, annual files, accessed 25 August 2026). The Chatham County segment carries a mix of long-distance travelers, local commuters, and heavy commercial truck traffic that generates a high volume of serious crashes.

Why I-95 is so dangerous near Savannah:

  • Speed and volume: Vehicles traveling at 70+ mph in dense traffic leave little margin for error. A momentary distraction at highway speed can result in a catastrophic crash.
  • Truck concentration: I-95 carries enormous volumes of freight traffic, and the clustering of trucks creates dangerous conditions for passenger vehicles, especially in construction zones and near interchange areas.
  • The I-95/I-16 interchange: This junction is a known bottleneck where merging traffic patterns produce frequent rear-end and sideswipe collisions.
  • Weather: Savannah's coastal climate produces sudden rain squalls that reduce visibility and create hydroplaning conditions on I-95's high-speed lanes.

Victims of I-95 crashes often face complex legal situations involving out-of-state trucking companies, multiple insurance carriers, and commercial vehicle regulations. An experienced Savannah car accident lawyer can navigate these complexities and ensure you pursue all available sources of compensation.

Abercorn Street: Savannah's Deadliest Road

Abercorn Street is the most dangerous road in the City of Savannah. Stretching from the Historic District south through Midtown and deep into the Southside suburbs, Abercorn carries enormous traffic volumes through a corridor lined with commercial development that generates constant turning movements, driveway conflicts, and parking lot access points.

Why Abercorn Street is so dangerous:

  • Commercial density: The Southside segment of Abercorn is lined with shopping centers, restaurants, and big-box retailers. Vehicles are constantly entering, exiting, and crossing Abercorn to access these businesses, creating an unending stream of conflict points.
  • High-crash intersections: Multiple Abercorn intersections rank among the city's most dangerous, including Abercorn at White Bluff Road, King George Boulevard, Rio Road, and Victory Drive (US-80).
  • Pedestrian danger: The wide, multi-lane roadway and the concentration of commercial destinations put pedestrians at significant risk, particularly near Oglethorpe Mall and the Abercorn/DeRenne intersection.
  • Speed transitions: Abercorn's character changes dramatically from a slow-moving historic district street to a high-speed suburban arterial, and the speed transitions catch drivers — especially tourists — off guard.

DeRenne Avenue: Narrow Lanes, Heavy Congestion

DeRenne Avenue is Savannah's primary east-west artery through the Midtown area, and it is notorious among local drivers for its dangerous conditions. The road carries traffic volumes that far exceed its design capacity, and the resulting congestion creates a pressure-cooker environment where aggressive driving and crashes are common.

Key danger factors:

  • Narrow lanes: DeRenne's lane widths are substandard by modern engineering criteria, giving drivers less room to maneuver and increasing the severity of sideswipe crashes.
  • Medical facility traffic: The proximity of Memorial Health University Medical Center to the DeRenne corridor generates heavy traffic including ambulances, medical transport vehicles, and visitors unfamiliar with the area.
  • Rush hour intensity: The 4:00 PM to 6:00 PM evening commute is DeRenne Avenue's most dangerous window. Sudden stops and aggressive lane changes during heavy congestion produce a high rate of rear-end collisions.
  • The DeRenne/Truman Parkway intersection: This intersection handles massive volumes as commuters travel between Savannah's Eastside and Southside neighborhoods, and it consistently ranks among the city's top crash locations.

I-516 (Lynes Parkway): High-Speed Danger

I-516, also known as Lynes Parkway, connects downtown Savannah to I-16 and the Southside. Despite being a relatively short highway, I-516 has a disproportionate history of serious crashes driven by high travel speeds, limited shoulders, and the merging patterns at its interchanges. Speeding is endemic on I-516, and the crashes that result from excessive speed tend to be severe. Motorcycle riders are especially vulnerable on I-516, where the high speed of surrounding traffic and limited visibility on the highway's curves create deadly conditions.

Common Causes of Highway Crashes in Savannah

  • Speeding: Excessive speed reduces stopping distance and dramatically increases crash severity. Georgia speed limits are governed by O.C.G.A. § 40-6-181, and the Georgia Super Speeder law (O.C.G.A. § 40-6-189.1) imposes additional penalties for drivers convicted of traveling 75+ mph on two-lane roads or 85+ mph on any road.
  • Truck driver fatigue: Federal hours-of-service regulations limit how long commercial truck drivers can operate without rest, but violations are common. Fatigued truck drivers on I-16 and I-95 pose an extreme danger to everyone sharing the road.
  • Distracted driving: Cell phone use, GPS navigation, and in-vehicle infotainment systems divert driver attention at highway speeds, where a few seconds of distraction can cover the length of a football field.
  • Impaired driving: Alcohol and drug impairment remains a leading cause of fatal highway crashes in Chatham County.
  • Tailgating: Following too closely on congested highways like I-16 and Abercorn Street eliminates the stopping distance needed to avoid rear-end collisions when traffic slows suddenly.
  • Poor road conditions: Potholes, worn lane markings, inadequate drainage, and construction zones all contribute to highway crashes. When a government entity's failure to maintain a road causes a crash, injured victims may have a claim under Georgia's governmental immunity exceptions.

Georgia and South Carolina Laws for Accident Victims

Statute of limitations: Georgia gives accident victims two years from the date of injury to file a personal injury lawsuit under O.C.G.A. § 9-3-33. South Carolina allows three years under S.C. Code § 15-3-530. Because I-95 crosses state lines, victims of crashes near the Georgia-South Carolina border need to be aware of both deadlines.

Comparative fault: Under O.C.G.A. § 51-12-33, Georgia follows a modified comparative fault rule allowing recovery if you are less than 50% at fault, with your award reduced by your percentage of responsibility. South Carolina's threshold is slightly more favorable — recovery is allowed if you are less than 51% at fault.

Commercial vehicle regulations: Truck accidents on I-16, I-95, and I-516 may involve violations of federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration regulations governing driver hours of service, vehicle maintenance, cargo securement, and driver qualifications. These violations can serve as powerful evidence of negligence in your injury claim.

Wrongful death: When a highway crash kills a victim, Georgia's wrongful death statute (O.C.G.A. § 51-4-1) allows the surviving spouse, children, or parents to pursue compensation. South Carolina's wrongful death statute (S.C. Code § 15-51-10) provides similar rights.

What to Do After a Highway Crash in Savannah

  1. Move to safety if possible. Highway crashes create extreme secondary collision risks. If your vehicle is drivable, move it to the shoulder. Turn on hazard lights and stay behind the guardrail.
  2. Call 911 immediately. Georgia law requires reporting accidents involving injuries. Highway crashes often require specialized response including fire rescue and traffic control.
  3. Seek medical treatment even if you think your injuries are minor. Adrenaline can mask pain from serious injuries like concussions, internal bleeding, and soft tissue damage.
  4. Document the scene with photographs if it is safe to do so. Capture vehicle positions, road conditions, weather, skid marks, and any debris patterns.
  5. Do not discuss fault with other drivers or insurance representatives at the scene.
  6. Contact a Savannah car accident lawyer before providing recorded statements to any insurance company.
