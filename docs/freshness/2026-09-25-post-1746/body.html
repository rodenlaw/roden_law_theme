Animal-vehicle collisions are far more common than many drivers realize. According to State Farm, drivers made more than 1.6 million animal-collision insurance claims nationwide between July 2025 and June 2026, and the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety counted 210 deaths in animal-collision crashes in 2024. In Georgia and South Carolina — where deer populations are substantial and rural highways are common — understanding how your car insurance applies to these accidents is critical for protecting yourself financially.

How Animal-Related Car Accidents Happen

Animal-vehicle collisions can involve everything from deer darting across a rural highway to livestock wandering onto an interstate. The most common types of animal-related accidents include:

  • Deer strikes — The most frequent animal collision in both Georgia and South Carolina, especially during fall mating season (October through December)
  • Livestock encounters — Cattle, horses, and other farm animals that escape fenced pastures and wander onto roads
  • Stray dog and cat collisions — Common in suburban and urban areas, particularly at dusk and dawn
  • Swerve-to-avoid accidents — Drivers who swerve to miss an animal and strike another vehicle, guardrail, tree, or ditch
  • Wild boar and feral hog crossings — A hazard in rural areas of both states

In State Farm's 2025–26 study, a Georgia driver's odds of an animal collision were 1 in 115 (22nd among states) and a South Carolina driver's 1 in 103 (18th), both higher than the national 1 in 145. According to SCDNR's 2024 Deer Harvest Report, the S.C. Department of Public Safety recorded 6,628 deer-vehicle collisions statewide in 2024 (preliminary).

Does Car Insurance Cover Animal Accidents?

Whether your car insurance covers an animal-related accident depends entirely on the type of coverage you carry and the specific circumstances of the collision.

Comprehensive Coverage

If you hit an animal directly — such as striking a deer that runs into the road — this is typically covered under your comprehensive insurance policy (sometimes called "other than collision" coverage). Comprehensive coverage pays to repair or replace your vehicle when damage results from events other than a collision with another vehicle. This includes animal strikes, falling objects, theft, vandalism, natural disasters, and fire.

Key points about comprehensive coverage for animal strikes:

  • You will typically pay your comprehensive deductible before coverage kicks in
  • A comprehensive claim is usually treated as not-at-fault and is less likely to raise your premium than an at-fault collision claim, though it can still affect claims-free discounts
  • Comprehensive coverage is optional unless your vehicle is financed or leased, in which case your lender typically requires it
  • If you only carry liability insurance (the minimum required by law), you have no coverage for animal strike damage to your own vehicle

Collision Coverage

If you swerve to avoid an animal and hit another object — a guardrail, tree, ditch, or another vehicle — the damage to your car is typically covered under collision coverage, not comprehensive. Collision claims may affect your insurance rates because the insurer may consider the swerve an at-fault incident. This distinction is important: directly hitting the deer is often the less costly insurance outcome compared to swerving and hitting something else.

Animal Accident Laws in Georgia and South Carolina

Georgia

Georgia follows a modified comparative fault system under O.C.G.A. § 51-12-33. If you are injured in a swerve-to-avoid accident caused by loose livestock or a negligently controlled animal, you can recover damages as long as you are less than 50% at fault.

Georgia's statute of limitations for personal injury claims is 2 years from the date of the accident (O.C.G.A. § 9-3-33). For property damage claims, the deadline is 4 years (O.C.G.A. § 9-3-31).

Georgia makes it unlawful for an owner to let livestock run at large on a public road (O.C.G.A. § 4-3-3). Liability is not automatic: you must show negligence, but an animal loose on the road lets a jury infer the owner was negligent unless the owner proves ordinary care in confining it. If cattle, horses, or other livestock escape due to the owner's negligence and cause a car accident, the animal owner may be held liable for your injuries and vehicle damage.

South Carolina

South Carolina also uses a modified comparative fault system. You can recover compensation if you are less than 51% at fault. The statute of limitations for personal injury claims is 3 years from the date of the accident (S.C. Code § 15-3-530).

South Carolina makes it unlawful for an owner wilfully or negligently to let any domestic animal run at large beyond the owner's own land (S.C. Code § 47-7-110). The South Carolina Supreme Court has held that an animal loose on the highway is not enough by itself; you must offer evidence from which a jury could find the owner negligent in letting it stray (Reed v. Clark, 277 S.C. 310 (1982)).

In both states, the minimum required auto insurance does not include comprehensive or collision coverage — only liability coverage, plus uninsured motorist coverage in South Carolina (S.C. Code § 38-77-150). Georgia requires minimum liability of 25/50/25 (O.C.G.A. § 33-7-11), while South Carolina requires 25/50/25 (S.C. Code § 38-77-140). Without optional comprehensive coverage, you have no insurance protection for animal strike damage to your own vehicle.

What to Do After Hitting an Animal

If you hit an animal while driving in Georgia or South Carolina, take these steps to protect your safety and your insurance claim:

  1. Pull over safely — Move your vehicle to the shoulder if possible. Turn on your hazard lights to alert other drivers
  2. Call 911 — Report the accident, especially if there are injuries, significant vehicle damage, or the animal is blocking the roadway. A police report strengthens your insurance claim. Georgia requires notifying police of any injury or apparent damage of $500 or more (O.C.G.A. § 40-6-273); South Carolina requires immediate notice of any injury (S.C. Code § 56-5-1260)
  3. Do not approach the animal — Injured animals can be dangerous and unpredictable. Wait for animal control or wildlife officers
  4. Document the scene — Photograph your vehicle damage, the animal (if safe to do so), road conditions, and the location. Note the time, visibility conditions, and weather
  5. Check for injuries — Seek medical attention for yourself and any passengers, even if injuries seem minor. Head injuries and whiplash may not present symptoms immediately
  6. Contact your insurance company — File a comprehensive claim (for direct animal strikes) or collision claim (for swerve accidents) promptly
  7. If livestock was involved — Try to identify the animal's owner. Check for brands, ear tags, or nearby farms. This information is critical if you need to pursue a liability claim against the owner

Swerving to Avoid an Animal: Who Is Liable?

Some of the most serious animal-related accidents occur not from hitting the animal itself, but from swerving to avoid it. When a driver swerves and strikes another vehicle, guardrail, or rolls into a ditch, determining fault becomes more complex.

In both Georgia and South Carolina, a driver who swerves may be found partially or fully at fault if their reaction was unreasonable under the circumstances. Both states recognize the sudden-emergency doctrine: a driver who reacts reasonably to an animal that appears without warning is not held to perfect judgment, but the doctrine does not protect a driver whose own speed or inattention created the emergency (Stephens v. Hypes, a Georgia deer-swerve case; Clark v. Cantrell in South Carolina). Courts generally consider:

  • The speed the driver was traveling
  • Whether the driver had time to stop rather than swerve
  • Road and weather conditions at the time
  • Whether the swerve was proportionate to the danger posed by the animal
  • Whether other vehicles were in adjacent lanes

If a multi-vehicle accident results from one driver swerving to avoid an animal, the comparative fault rules apply. An experienced car accident lawyer can help establish the chain of events and distribute fault appropriately.

Livestock on the Road: Open Range and Fence Laws

When livestock causes a highway accident, the animal owner may be liable under Georgia and South Carolina negligence principles. Key factors include:

  • Fence condition — Both states make it unlawful for an owner to let livestock run at large (O.C.G.A. § 4-3-3; S.C. Code § 47-7-110). A broken or poorly maintained fence is the usual evidence of negligence; a fence in good repair with closed gates has defeated claims
  • Prior escapes — If the animal had escaped before, the owner had notice of the fencing deficiency
  • Location — Animals near heavily trafficked roads create a higher duty of care
  • Time of day — Loose livestock at night on an unlit rural road is especially dangerous
