After a car accident, trucking collision, or any serious injury caused by someone else's negligence, you might assume that the settlement check your attorney secures is entirely yours to keep. In many cases, however, third parties hold legal claims against a portion of that money. These claims are called liens, and they can reduce the amount you actually take home by thousands — or even tens of thousands — of dollars.

Both Georgia and South Carolina allow specific categories of lien holders to recover money from personal injury settlements. Understanding which liens apply, how they work under each state's laws, and how to negotiate them down is critical to protecting your compensation. For background on lien law generally, see the Legal Information Institute's overview of liens.

What Is a Lien on a Personal Injury Settlement?

A lien is a legal right held by a third party to claim a portion of your settlement or verdict proceeds. When you receive medical treatment, government benefits, or workers' compensation payments after an injury, the entities that paid those costs often have a statutory or contractual right to be reimbursed from any recovery you receive from the at-fault party.

Liens can come from hospitals, health insurers, Medicare, Medicaid, the VA, workers' compensation carriers, and even your own attorney. In both states a valid lien follows the settlement money, and your attorney must hold disputed funds in trust until it is resolved.

The key distinction is between a statutory lien — created by state or federal law — and a contractual lien — created by an insurance policy or provider agreement. Statutory liens carry the force of law and are harder to negotiate. Contractual liens, while enforceable, sometimes offer more room for reduction.

If you were injured in a truck accident or motorcycle crash that required extensive hospitalization, the total lien amount can be substantial. Cases involving traumatic brain injuries or spinal cord injuries often generate six-figure medical bills — and corresponding liens that must be addressed before you see a dime.

Medical Provider Liens

Medical provider liens are among the most common liens in personal injury cases. Hospitals, physicians, and other treatment providers can claim part of your settlement to recover the cost of care they provided for your injuries.

Georgia Hospital Liens

Under O.C.G.A. § 44-14-470, a hospital, nursing home, or physician, chiropractic or burn-care practice that treats you has a lien on your claim for the reasonable charges for that care, subordinate to your attorney's lien.

To perfect it, the provider must give written notice at least 15 days before filing a verified statement with the superior court clerk — within 75 days after discharge for hospitals, or 90 days after first treatment for physician and chiropractic practices. Since July 1, 2023, the lien is unenforceable unless the provider first billed your health insurer. Georgia hospital liens do not apply to workers' compensation claims — only to third-party liability recoveries — and cannot include charges for unrelated conditions.

South Carolina Hospital Liens

South Carolina has no hospital-lien statute. A hospital or doctor there cannot file a lien against your claim; its right to be paid rests on your contract with it, including any assignment of benefits or letter of protection you signed, and is enforced like any other bill. The claims that do reach a South Carolina settlement are health-insurer subrogation, Medicaid, Medicare and workers' compensation, each covered below.

Doctor and Physician Liens

In Georgia, physician and chiropractic practices hold the same statutory lien as hospitals, under the 90-day rule above. In South Carolina, a doctor's claim is contractual — typically a letter of protection under which the doctor agrees to wait for payment until the case resolves — and contractual claims tend to be more negotiable than statutory liens. The enforceability of a physician lien depends on the specific language of the signed agreement and whether the provider followed proper procedures.

Health Insurance Liens and Subrogation Rights

If your health insurer paid for injury-related treatment, it may have a right to be reimbursed from your settlement, within statutory limits in both states. This right is called subrogation, and it functions much like a lien.

Subrogation works like this: your insurer pays your medical bills, then steps into your shoes and demands reimbursement from your settlement. The legal basis is usually found in the insurance policy's subrogation or reimbursement clause.

Georgia Subrogation Rules

Georgia codifies the "made whole" doctrine in O.C.G.A. § 33-24-56.1: a health or disability insurer may be reimbursed only if your recovery exceeds all of your economic and noneconomic losses, and even then its claim is reduced by a pro rata share of your attorney's fees and expenses. If your settlement does not fully cover your medical bills, lost wages, and pain and suffering, the insurer has no right of reimbursement.

A Georgia-regulated policy cannot contract around this rule. The exceptions are plans state law cannot reach: self-funded employer (ERISA) plans, federal-employee plans, Medicare, Medicaid and TRICARE. A self-funded ERISA plan's own terms control, but it can reach only the settlement fund itself.

South Carolina Subrogation Rules

South Carolina's protection is statutory. Under S.C. Code § 38-71-190, a health insurer may subrogate only if the policy provides for it and only up to what it paid, must pay its share of fees and costs, and can be barred by the Director of Insurance from inequitable subrogation. The type of health insurance you carry — private, employer-sponsored, ERISA, or government — plays a major role in determining how much of your settlement a health insurer can claim.

Government Liens — Medicare, Medicaid, and VA

Government liens are the most powerful and least negotiable category of liens in personal injury cases. Federal law gives Medicare, Medicaid, and the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) recovery rights that state law cannot cut off, although federal law also caps what Medicaid can take.

Medicare Liens

Under the Medicare Secondary Payer Act (42 U.S.C. § 1395y(b)), Medicare must be repaid from any liability settlement, judgment or award, whether or not it mentions medical expenses. This federal right overrides state lien and made-whole rules and can be reduced, waived or compromised only through Medicare's own procedures; CMS does not pursue physical-trauma liability settlements of $750 or less.

Medicare's lien applies to conditional payments — bills Medicare paid on the condition that it be repaid if a liable party later pays. According to CMS, its recovery center lists the payments it considers related, lets you dispute unrelated charges, and after settlement issues a formal demand; repayment is due within 60 days, interest runs from the demand date, and unpaid debts go to the Treasury for offset. If funds are paid out first, Medicare can collect from you or your attorney, and double damages are possible if the government must sue. The demand is reduced by Medicare's share of your attorney's fees and costs.

Medicaid Liens

Both Georgia and South Carolina Medicaid programs have statutory rights to recover payments from personal injury settlements. Georgia's Medicaid lien authority comes from O.C.G.A. § 49-4-149, while South Carolina's derives from S.C. Code §§ 43-7-430 and 43-7-440. Like Medicare, Medicaid liens are backed by federal law (42 U.S.C. § 1396a(a)(25)) and cannot simply be ignored.

However, in Arkansas Dept. of Health & Human Services v. Ahlborn (2006), the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that Medicaid can only assert a lien against the portion of a settlement representing medical expenses — not the entire recovery. This ruling provides a basis for reducing Medicaid liens, especially when non-medical damages like pain and suffering make up the bulk of the settlement.

VA Liens

Veterans treated by the VA, and service members and families on TRICARE, may face a federal recovery claim under the Federal Medical Care Recovery Act (42 U.S.C. § 2651) and 38 U.S.C. § 1729. The United States can recover the reasonable value of the care from the at-fault party or its insurer.

Workers' Compensation Liens

When a workplace injury involves a negligent third party — such as a commercial truck driver who causes a crash while you are on the job — you may receive both workers' compensation benefits and a third-party settlement. The workers' compensation carrier then has a lien on your third-party recovery.

Georgia Workers' Compensation Liens

Under O.C.G.A. § 34-9-11.1, a Georgia workers' compensation insurer that has paid benefits to an injured worker is entitled to a lien on any recovery the worker obtains from a negligent third party. The lien covers the disability, death and medical benefits actually paid, but it is enforceable only if the carrier proves you have been fully and completely compensated for all economic and noneconomic losses, counting both the comp benefits and the third-party recovery.

If the carrier does recover, the court may apportion a reasonable attorney's fee between your lawyer and the carrier's lawyer by the work each did.

South Carolina Workers' Compensation Liens

S.C. Code § 42-1-560 governs workers' compensation liens in South Carolina. The carrier's lien is on the recovery net of the fees and expenses of obtaining it. Notice of suit goes to the Commission, employer and carrier within 30 days, and the suit must be started within one year after the carrier accepts liability or pays under an award, or the claim can pass to the carrier. If a settlement is less than your total damages, the Commission can cut the lien proportionally.

Workers' compensation liens are particularly relevant in slip and fall cases that occur on a job site, construction injuries involving third-party contractors, and motor vehicle accidents that happen during the course of employment.

Attorney Liens

Your own attorney also has a claim on the proceeds for fees and costs — the attorney's lien, which differs by state.

Georgia Attorney Liens

Georgia recognizes both charging liens and retaining liens under O.C.G.A. § 15-19-14. A charging lien gives the attorney a claim against the settlement proceeds obtained through the attorney's efforts. In personal injury cases, it secures the contingency fee set in your written fee agreement plus any case costs advanced by the attorney.

South Carolina Attorney Liens

South Carolina has no attorney's-lien statute. Its courts recognize a common-law lien for costs the attorney advanced; a lien for the fee itself exists only if your written fee agreement creates one (Eleazer v. Hardaway Concrete Co.).

In Georgia the attorney's lien outranks every lien except tax liens, and hospital and Medicaid liens are expressly subordinate to it; Medicare is not subordinate but reduces its demand for fees and costs.

These differences matter in practice. A Georgia lien that misses the notice or filing rules is invalid, except against a liable party or insurer that had actual notice of it before settling, and comparative fault findings reduce your total settlement — which also reduces what lien holders can collect. In Georgia, you are barred from recovery if you are 50% or more at fault under O.C.G.A. § 51-12-33. In South Carolina, the threshold is slightly more favorable — you can recover as long as your fault stays below 51%.

How to Negotiate and Reduce Liens on Your Settlement

Most liens can be reduced. Some reductions are written into the statutes; others come from challenging the lien's validity or amount. Here are the primary strategies:

Challenge the Lien's Validity

If a Georgia provider missed the filing deadline, included charges for unrelated treatment, failed to serve the correct parties, or never billed your health insurer first, the lien may be invalid. Your attorney should review every lien for procedural defects before agreeing to pay anything.

Apply the Made Whole Doctrine

In Georgia, the complete-compensation rule eliminates a health insurer's reimbursement claim if your settlement does not fully compensate you; in South Carolina, you can petition the Director of Insurance to disallow inequitable subrogation. This argument is especially powerful in catastrophic injury cases — such as those involving traumatic brain injuries or spinal cord damage — where the total damages far exceed the available insurance coverage.

Request a Compromise from Government Lien Holders

Medicare reduces its demand for your attorney's fees and costs, and you can request a waiver or compromise, with detailed documentation of the case value and the reasons a full recovery was not possible, or appeal the amount. South Carolina Medicaid must cut its claim by 25 percent when you hired a lawyer and may compromise the rest; Georgia Medicaid is limited to the medical-expense share of the recovery.

Negotiate with the Workers' Compensation Carrier

Workers' compensation carriers frequently agree to reduce their liens in exchange for faster resolution. Many will accept an additional reduction beyond the statutory fee offset rather than risk losing their recovery entirely at trial.

Use the "Common Fund" Doctrine

This doctrine holds that a lien holder who benefits from the attorney's efforts should bear a proportionate share of fees and costs. For most liens the share is written into the statute: Georgia health insurers, Medicare, and South Carolina workers' compensation carriers, health insurers and Medicaid all recover net of fees and costs, and the reduction generally tracks your actual fee rather than a fixed one-third.

How a Personal Injury Lawyer Protects Your Settlement

The difference between an attorney who aggressively negotiates liens and one who pays them at face value can be tens of thousands of dollars in your pocket. Here is what an experienced personal injury attorney does to protect your recovery:

  • Identifies all liens early. Your attorney tracks every potential lien holder — hospitals, insurers, Medicare, Medicaid, workers' comp carriers — and obtains lien amounts before settlement negotiations begin.
  • Audits lien amounts for accuracy. Medical provider liens often include billing errors or charges for unrelated treatment. Your attorney reviews every line item and challenges anything that does not belong.
  • Negotiates reductions. Most lien holders accept less than the full amount when presented with a well-documented request.
  • Applies legal protections. Georgia's made whole rule, South Carolina's subrogation limits, and statutory fee reductions work in your favor — but only if your attorney raises them.
  • Ensures proper distribution. Settlement funds are held in trust and distributed according to lien priority. Failing to properly satisfy liens can expose both you and your attorney to legal liability.

This applies across all injury types — car accidents, medical malpractice, wrongful death, and beyond. Lien resolution is also time-sensitive. Georgia's two-year statute of limitations (O.C.G.A. § 9-3-33) and South Carolina's three-year deadline (S.C. Code § 15-3-530) mean that delays from unresolved liens can put your entire case at risk.
