One of the most common concerns people have after suffering an injury in a car accident, truck collision, or workplace incident is whether they can afford to hire a lawyer. The contingency fee system removes that barrier entirely. Under this arrangement, your attorney does not charge any fees upfront — instead, the lawyer's payment comes as a percentage of the compensation recovered in your case. If your case does not result in a settlement or verdict, you owe nothing for legal representation. The Legal Information Institute at Cornell Law School provides a helpful overview of how contingency fees operate in the American legal system.

What Is a Contingency Fee?

A contingency fee is a payment arrangement between a client and an attorney in which the attorney's fee is contingent upon — that is, depends on — winning the case. Unlike hourly billing or flat-fee arrangements common in other areas of law, the contingency fee model means:

  • No retainer — you do not pay a lump sum upfront to hire the attorney
  • No hourly billing — the attorney does not track hours and bill you monthly
  • Payment only from recovery — the attorney receives a pre-agreed percentage of the settlement or verdict
  • No recovery, no fee — if the case is unsuccessful, the attorney earns nothing for their time and effort

This system exists specifically to ensure that injury victims have access to legal representation regardless of their financial situation. Whether you are facing mounting medical bills from a motorcycle accident or have lost income due to a workplace injury, the contingency fee model means you can pursue justice without taking on additional financial risk.

How the Contingency Fee System Works

The contingency fee process follows a clear sequence:

Step 1: Free Initial Consultation

Most personal injury attorneys offer a free case evaluation where they review the facts of your accident, assess the strength of your claim, and explain your legal options. If the attorney believes you have a viable case, they will offer to represent you under a contingency fee agreement.

Step 2: Signing the Contingency Fee Agreement

This written contract spells out the exact terms — including the fee percentage, how expenses are handled, and what happens if the case is unsuccessful. In both Georgia and South Carolina, this agreement must be in writing; South Carolina also requires the client to sign it.

Step 3: The Attorney Handles Your Case

Your attorney investigates the accident, gathers evidence, negotiates with insurance companies, and — if necessary — files a lawsuit and takes the case to trial. Throughout this process, you pay nothing out of pocket for the attorney's time.

Step 4: Settlement or Verdict

If the case results in a settlement or jury verdict, the attorney's fee is calculated as a percentage of the total recovery. The fee, along with any case expenses, is deducted from the settlement before the remaining funds are distributed to you.

Step 5: If the Case Is Unsuccessful

If no compensation is recovered, you owe the attorney nothing for their legal services. The attorney absorbs the cost of the time invested in your case; whether you owe advanced expenses depends on the agreement — at Roden Law, there are no fees or costs with no recovery.

What "No Upfront Costs" Really Means

When a personal injury law firm advertises "no upfront costs," this typically means two things:

No attorney fees upfront: You do not pay for the lawyer's time until and unless the case is won. This is the core contingency fee promise.

The firm advances case expenses: Personal injury cases involve costs beyond attorney time — filing fees, medical record retrieval, expert witness fees, court reporter fees, accident reconstruction costs, and more. Under most contingency fee arrangements, the law firm advances these expenses and recoups them from the settlement or verdict. If the case is unsuccessful, many firms absorb these costs entirely, though this varies by firm and should be clarified in your fee agreement.

This is why it is essential to read your contingency fee agreement carefully and ask questions about expense handling before signing.

Typical Contingency Fee Percentages

Contingency fees in personal injury cases are commonly one-third (33.3%) of the recovery, often higher if a lawsuit is filed or the case is tried:

Stage of CaseCommon FeeExplanation
Pre-litigation settlement33.3% (one-third)Case settles before a lawsuit is filed
Post-filing / pre-trial33.3% – 40%Lawsuit is filed; case settles during litigation
Trial or appealOften 40%Case requires full trial preparation and/or courtroom presentation

Workers' compensation fees follow separate rules: in Georgia they need State Board of Workers' Compensation approval and are capped at 25% (O.C.G.A. § 34-9-108), and in South Carolina they are up to 33.3% with Commission approval (S.C. Code Regs. 67-1205).

The fee percentage often increases as the case progresses because the attorney's investment of time, resources, and risk increases at each stage. A case that settles quickly requires fewer resources than one that goes through discovery, depositions, expert testimony, and a multi-day trial.

Case Expenses vs. Attorney Fees

It is important to understand the difference between attorney fees and case expenses:

Attorney fees are the percentage of the recovery that compensates the lawyer for their legal work — strategy, negotiation, legal research, court appearances, and client communication.

Case expenses (also called "costs" or "disbursements") are the out-of-pocket costs associated with building and prosecuting the case:

  • Court filing fees — required to initiate a lawsuit
  • Medical record retrieval — fees charged by hospitals and doctors to copy medical files
  • Expert witness fees — payments to medical experts, accident reconstructionists, economists, and vocational specialists
  • Deposition costs — court reporter fees for recording sworn testimony
  • Process server fees — costs of formally delivering legal documents to defendants
  • Investigation costs — hiring investigators, obtaining police reports, and gathering surveillance footage
  • Postage and copying — administrative costs throughout the case

Most personal injury firms advance these costs and deduct them from the settlement. Whether expenses are deducted before or after the attorney fee is calculated can significantly affect your net recovery — always clarify this in your fee agreement.

Contingency Fee Rules in Georgia and South Carolina

Georgia Rules

Georgia's Rules of Professional Conduct (Georgia Rule 1.5) govern contingency fee arrangements. Key requirements include:

  • Written agreement required: All contingency fee agreements must be in writing and state the percentage(s) for settlement, trial or appeal (Rule 1.5(c)(1))
  • Fee must be reasonable: The fee percentage must be reasonable in light of the complexity, risk, and amount of work involved
  • Prohibited cases: Contingency fees are not permitted in criminal defense cases, or in domestic relations matters when the fee depends on securing a divorce or on the amount of alimony, support or a property settlement
  • Expense disclosure: The agreement must state the litigation and other expenses to be deducted from the recovery and whether they are deducted before or after the fee is calculated
  • Statute of limitations: Georgia's 2-year statute of limitations for personal injury claims (O.C.G.A. § 9-3-33) makes it important to consult an attorney promptly — delaying can forfeit your right to file
  • Comparative fault: Under O.C.G.A. § 51-12-33, your recovery is reduced by your percentage of fault, and you cannot recover if you are 50% or more at fault

South Carolina Rules

South Carolina's Rules of Professional Conduct (Rule 1.5) impose similar requirements with some differences:

  • Written agreement required: Must be signed by the client and state the percentage(s) for settlement, trial or appeal, the expenses to be deducted, whether they come off before or after the fee, and any expenses the client will be expected to pay
  • Reasonableness standard: South Carolina courts apply a reasonableness test considering the complexity, amount involved, and customary fees in the locality
  • Prohibited cases: Same restrictions on criminal and most domestic relations cases
  • Client's right to terminate: A client can terminate the contingency fee arrangement at any time, though the attorney may be entitled to compensation for work already performed (quantum meruit)
  • Statute of limitations: South Carolina's 3-year statute of limitations (S.C. Code § 15-3-530) provides more time but should not encourage delay
  • Comparative fault: South Carolina bars recovery only if you are 51% or more at fault — slightly more favorable than Georgia's threshold

Advantages of the Contingency Fee System

The contingency fee model offers significant advantages for accident victims:

Access to Justice

Most accident victims cannot afford to pay a lawyer by the hour — often hundreds of dollars an hour — while simultaneously dealing with medical bills, lost wages, and recovery. The contingency fee system ensures that financial resources do not determine who can obtain quality legal representation. Whether your claim involves a slip and fall, medical malpractice, or a wrongful death, the contingency fee system levels the playing field.

Aligned Incentives

Because the attorney only gets paid when you get paid, their financial interests are directly aligned with yours. Your attorney is motivated to maximize your recovery because their fee is a percentage of that recovery. This creates a partnership where both you and your lawyer benefit from the best possible outcome.

Risk Transfer

The attorney assumes the financial risk of the case. For example, if a case takes two years and $50,000 in expenses but ultimately fails, a firm that does not charge costs on a loss — as Roden Law does not — absorbs that loss, not you. This risk-sharing arrangement gives injury victims the ability to pursue legitimate claims without fear of financial ruin.

Quality Screening

Because attorneys bear the risk of losing their investment, they are selective about which cases they accept on contingency. If an experienced personal injury attorney agrees to take your case, it is a strong signal that they believe your claim has merit. This screening function benefits both clients and the legal system.

Questions to Ask Before Signing a Contingency Fee Agreement

Before entering a contingency fee arrangement, ask your attorney these important questions:

  1. What is the fee percentage, and does it change if the case goes to trial? Understand the fee structure at every stage of the case.
  2. Are case expenses deducted before or after the attorney fee? This affects how much you ultimately receive.
  3. What happens to expenses if the case is unsuccessful? Some firms absorb costs; others require reimbursement.
  4. Are there any costs I might be responsible for regardless of outcome? Clarify any exceptions to the "no fee unless we win" promise.
  5. How will I receive updates on my case and expenses? Establish expectations for communication and financial transparency.
  6. Can I terminate the agreement, and what are the consequences? Understand your right to change attorneys if needed.
  7. Will the settlement check come through a trust account? Ethical rules require attorneys to hold settlement funds in a client trust account and provide a detailed disbursement statement.
