If you drive through North Charleston, you probably already know the intersection of Ashley Phosphate Road and I-26 feels dangerous. What you may not know is that data confirms it: 629 crashes were recorded there between 2011 and 2015 — roughly one every three days, and more than at any other intersection in Berkeley, Charleston or Dorchester counties. Statewide it ranked second for 2011–2015, behind I-20 at US-176 in Columbia; in 2024 it ranked fifth. Notably, none of those 629 crashes was fatal, though 181 people were injured (Post and Courier 2017 analysis of preliminary South Carolina Department of Public Safety data, 2011–2015).

As personal injury lawyers who handle crash cases from this intersection regularly, we've seen the injuries it produces — from T-bone collisions to rear-end chain reactions. Here's what makes this intersection so crash-prone and what you can do to protect yourself.

The Numbers: Ashley Phosphate & I-26 by the Data

Let's put the danger in perspective:

  • 2024 (latest state data): 174 collisions at I-26 and Ashley Phosphate Road — second in Charleston County and the tri-county behind I-26 at US-78 (190) and fifth statewide. No one was killed; 39 people were injured, and 148 of the crashes were property damage only (SCDPS 2024 Fact Book)

5 Reasons This Intersection Is So Crash-Prone

1. Interstate Speed Meets Red Lights

Vehicles exiting I-26 at Exit 209 come off the interstate and reach a signal at Ashley Phosphate Road a short distance later. Drivers who are distracted, unfamiliar with the area, or simply carrying too much speed can run the red light. The resulting T-bone collision with a vehicle legally entering the intersection produces catastrophic force.

2. Complex Left-Turn Movements

Ashley Phosphate Road has multiple left-turn lanes in both directions. Vehicles turning left must cross three lanes of opposing traffic on a seven-lane road. The margin for error is razor-thin — misjudge the gap by even one second and the result is a violent head-on-angle collision combining the energy of both vehicles.

3. Commercial Density Around the Interchange

Shopping centers, hotels, gas stations, and restaurants line Ashley Phosphate Road around the interchange. Drivers aren't just passing through — they're turning, merging, exiting parking lots, and hunting for entrances. This constant churn of turning movements creates unpredictable conflicts that even attentive drivers struggle to anticipate.

4. Drivers Who Fail to Yield

According to the SCDPS 2024 Fact Book, failure to yield the right of way was the leading contributing factor in Charleston County collisions in 2024. Where off-ramp traffic, left turns, and driveway traffic all meet, one driver who fails to yield can cause a collision.

5. Volume Has Outgrown Design Capacity

According to SCDOT, traffic on Ashley Phosphate Road has grown beyond the recommended level for two-way left-turn lanes. North Charleston's population reached about 129,245 in July 2025 (Census estimate), and I-26 carries 170,500 vehicles a day between Ashley Phosphate Road and Aviation Avenue, with 65,000 more on Ashley Phosphate Road (SCDOT, 2025).

What Should Be Done About It

Traffic engineers have tools that could reduce the crash frequency at Ashley Phosphate/I-26:

  • Protected left-turn signals — Eliminating permissive left turns during high-volume periods
  • Red-light cameras — A federal study found about 25% fewer right-angle crashes but about 15% more rear-end crashes; South Carolina law currently bars local camera ticketing (S.C. Code § 56-5-710(B))
  • Extended deceleration lanes — Giving I-26 exit traffic more distance to slow before reaching the signal
  • Restricted access management — Reducing the number of driveway access points near the intersection
  • Raised medians — SCDOT broke ground in July 2025 on an Ashley Phosphate Road safety project (Rivers Avenue to Dorchester Road) adding raised medians, high-visibility crosswalks, and better lighting

SCDOT has begun with that raised-median project; until more of these improvements are in place, crashes here are likely to continue.

What to Do If You're Injured at This Intersection

  1. Call 911 — Get a police report documenting the scene, signal state, and contributing factors
  2. Get medical treatment immediately — Trident Medical Center (9330 Medical Plaza Dr) is the closest trauma facility
  3. Photograph everything — Signal positions, vehicle damage, intersection layout, your injuries
  4. Get witness contacts — Nearby businesses may have seen the crash; get names and numbers before they leave
  5. Do not give recorded statements — The other driver's insurance company will try to minimize your claim
  6. Contact a lawyer — An attorney can pull traffic camera footage, obtain the signal timing report, and preserve evidence before it's lost

Your Legal Rights Under South Carolina Law

If you were injured at Ashley Phosphate Road and I-26:

  • Statute of limitations: 3 years from the date of injury to file suit (S.C. Code § 15-3-530)
  • Comparative fault: You can recover damages if you were less than 51% at fault — your award is reduced by your percentage of responsibility
  • Government liability: If a malfunctioning or defective traffic signal contributed to your crash and the responsible agency knew or should have known and did not fix it, SCDOT or another agency may be liable under the South Carolina Tort Claims Act (S.C. Code § 15-78-60(15)) — highway design itself is immune, and damages are capped at $300,000 per person and $600,000 per occurrence (S.C. Code § 15-78-120). These claims also have their own deadline: suit within two years, or three years if a verified claim is filed with the agency within one year (S.C. Code §§ 15-78-110, 15-78-80); filing that claim is optional
